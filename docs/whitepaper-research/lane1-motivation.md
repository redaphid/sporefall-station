## 1. Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006) - SDT and games, PENS

**Citation:** Ryan, R. M., Rigby, C. S., & Przybylski, A. (2006). The motivational pull of video games: A self-determination theory approach. *Motivation and Emotion, 30*(4), 344-360. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8

**Opened:**
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8 (confirmed title, authors, 2006, journal, 30(4), 344-360)
- https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf (full text; header shows journal, DOI, authors, 2006)

**Method / samples (from full text):** four studies. Study 1: 89 undergraduates (23 male, 66 female), 20-min play of Super Mario 64. Study 2: 50 undergraduates, two games on two visits. Study 3: 58 undergraduates, four N64 games, 40-min sessions, multilevel model (games nested in persons). Study 4: survey of 730 members of an online games community (679 male, 51 female, age 16-44, mean 22.1) with MMO experience.

**PENS subscales used here:** In-Game Competence (5 items), In-Game Autonomy (5 items), Presence (9 items: physical, emotional, narrative), Intuitive Controls (3 items); Relatedness added in Study 4.

**Key quotes:**
- Quote (abstract): "perceived in-game autonomy and competence are associated with game enjoyment, preferences, and changes in well-being pre- to post-play. Competence and autonomy perceptions are also related to the intuitive nature of game controls"
- Quote (abstract): "SDT's theorized needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness independently predict enjoyment and future game play."
- Quote (autonomy): "we expect autonomy to be enhanced by game designs that provide considerable flexibility over movement and strategies, choice over tasks and goals, and those where rewards are structured so as to provide feedback rather than to control the player's behavior."
- Quote (competence): "Perceived competence would thus be enhanced in gaming contexts where game controls are intuitive and readily mastered, and tasks within the game provide ongoing optimal challenges and opportunities for positive feedback."
- Quote (PENS competence item): "The game kept me on my toes but did not overwhelm me."
- Quote (intuitive controls): "whether they make sense, are easily mastered, and do not interfere with once [sic] sense of being in the game"
- Quote (Study 1): "Intuitive controls were related to both competence and autonomy, which mediated the relations of IC to outcomes."
- Quote (Study 4): "relatedness also emerges as an important satisfaction that promotes a sense of presence, game enjoyment, and an intention for future play."

**Paraphrase:** Enjoyment and wanting to play again track how competent and self-directed the player feels in the game. Intuitive controls help mostly by raising felt competence and autonomy. In multiplayer, relatedness adds its own effect. PENS need scores predicted outcomes beyond Yee's motive factors.

**For Sporefall:** Autonomy means real choice over build and strategy, so one dominant build (the playtest finding) is an autonomy failure, not just a balance issue. Competence needs play that keeps players on their toes without overwhelming them: floor-1 enemies that never threaten give no competence signal. Kids who cannot read what their build does lack the feedback that competence depends on. Controller-only play for 8-12 year olds makes the Intuitive Controls subscale directly relevant. Co-op with an adult relative maps to relatedness (Study 4 evidence is from adult MMO players, not kids).

## 2. Przybylski, Rigby & Ryan (2010) - motivational model of engagement

**Citation:** Przybylski, A. K., Rigby, C. S., & Ryan, R. M. (2010). A motivational model of video game engagement. *Review of General Psychology, 14*(2), 154-166. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019440

**Opened:**
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/a0019440 (confirmed title, authors, 2010, journal, 14(2), 154-166, abstract)
- https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2010_PrzybylskiRigbyRyan_ROGP.pdf (full text)

**Type:** theoretical review of the authors' SDT-based studies (not a single new empirical study).

**Key quotes:**
- Quote (abstract): "both the appeal and well-being effects of video games are based in their potential to satisfy basic psychological needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness."
- Quote (competence): "if the challenges underwhelmed players, they would lead to boredom, and if they overwhelmed the player, they would generate frustration."
- Quote (competence): "Because skill-graded challenges and positive feedback are key to player experiences of competence need satisfaction (Ryan & Deci, 2000a), they represent an important element in motivating play."
- Quote (autonomy): "supported autonomy giving players options over multiple game elements: what missions they choose, the skills they acquire, and how their characters appear."
- Quote (mastery of controls): "Mastery of controls plays an important role in game motivation, largely as a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for achieving psychologically need-satisfying play."
- Quote (mastery results): "it no longer accounted for unique variance in player motivation and well-being when in-game need satisfaction was considered."
- Quote (feedback design): "Games that provide performance feedback in ways that emphasize how the player excelled and suggest tactics for future improvement could serve to enhance need satisfaction, whereas feedback communicated only in terms of what went wrong (or who the player performed worse than) is likely undermining."

**Paraphrase:** The PENS model names three needs (competence, autonomy, relatedness) plus two supporting factors (mastery of controls, immersion). Mastery of controls is a gate: needed to reach need satisfaction, but explains nothing extra once need satisfaction is measured. Competence comes from difficulty matched to skill plus positive, informative feedback. Autonomy comes from meaningful options, such as which skills you acquire.

**For Sporefall:** Control mastery for kids on a controller is a precondition to get right early, but it is not what makes the game fun. The build system is the autonomy lever ("the skills they acquire"). Build-readability feedback should tell a kid what worked and suggest a tactic (for example, that frost slowed the boss), not only what failed. The "future directions" feedback claim is a proposal, not a tested finding.

## 3. Csikszentmihalyi (1990) - Flow (book record only)

**Citation:** Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). *Flow: The psychology of optimal experience* (1st ed.). New York: Harper & Row. xii, 303 p. ISBN 0-06-016253-8. LCCN 89045645. OCLC 20392741.

**Opened:**
- https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0060162538.json (edition record: title, subtitle, "Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi.", Harper & Row, New York, 1990, 1st ed., xii + 303 p., LCCN, OCLC)
- https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=flow+the+psychology+of+optimal+experience+csikszentmihalyi (work record, first_publish_year 1990, 25 editions, later Harper Perennial reprints)

**Status:** bibliographic record verified. Book text NOT opened (library copy is lend-only), so no direct quote from the book here. The flow elements below come from Sweetser & Wyeth's (2005) summary of the book (entry 4), i.e. secondary.

**Flow elements as summarized by Sweetser & Wyeth (2005), citing Csikszentmihalyi 1990** (quote from their text): "(1) a task that can be completed; (2) the ability to concentrate on the task; (3) that concentration is possible because the task has clear goals; (4) that concentration is possible because the task provides immediate feedback; (5) the ability to exercise a sense of control over actions; (6) a deep but effortless involvement that removes awareness of the frustrations of everyday life; (7) concern for self disappears, but sense of self emerges stronger afterwards; and (8) the sense of the duration of time is altered." Plus precursor: "a match between the person's skills and the challenges associated with the task, with both being over a certain level."

**For Sporefall:** Cite the book for the concept; cite primary page numbers only after someone checks a physical or library copy.

## 4. Sweetser & Wyeth (2005) - GameFlow

**Citation:** Sweetser, P., & Wyeth, P. (2005). GameFlow: A model for evaluating player enjoyment in games. *Computers in Entertainment, 3*(3), Article 3 (QUT ePrints lists pp. 1-24). https://doi.org/10.1145/1077246.1077253

**Opened:**
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/1077246.1077253 (title, both authors, July 2005, Computers in Entertainment 3(3), article 3, abstract)
- https://eprints.qut.edu.au/44776/ (repository record: 2005, 3(3), pp. 1-24, DOI; no full text there)
- https://www.valuesatplay.org/wp-content/uploads/2007/09/sweetser.pdf (full text, header "ACM Computers in Entertainment, Vol. 3, No. 3, July 2005. Article 3A.", authors at The University of Queensland). This is a third-party hosted copy; ACM DL returned 403.

**The eight GameFlow elements (Table II, each with a goal):**
1. Concentration: "Games should require concentration and the player should be able to concentrate on the game"
2. Challenge: "Games should be sufficiently challenging and match the player's skill level"
3. Player Skills: "Games must support player skill development and mastery"
4. Control: "Players should feel a sense of control over their actions in the game"
5. Clear Goals: "Games should provide the player with clear goals at appropriate times"
6. Feedback: "Players must receive appropriate feedback at appropriate times"
7. Immersion: "Players should experience deep but effortless involvement in the game"
8. Social Interaction: "Games should support and create opportunities for social interaction" (does not map to a flow element)

**Selected criteria (quotes from Table II):**
- Challenge: "the level of challenge should increase as the player progresses through the game and increases their skill level"; "games should provide new challenges at an appropriate pace"
- Player Skills: "players should be taught to play the game through tutorials or initial levels that feel like playing the game"; "game interfaces and mechanics should be easy to learn and use"
- Control: "players should feel a sense of control over the actions that they take and the strategies that they use and that they are free to play the game the way that they want (not simply discovering actions and strategies planned by the game developers)"
- Feedback: "players should receive immediate feedback on their actions"; "players should always know their status or score"
- Social: "games should support competition and cooperation between players"
- Challenge text: "If the challenges are greater than the skills, the result is anxiety; if the challenges are less than the skills, the result is apathy"

**Method:** literature synthesis of game usability/UX heuristics mapped onto flow; validated by expert review of two RTS games, Warcraft 3 (avg 94% across 51 professional reviews) and Lords of EverQuest (avg 61% across 32), each criterion scored 0-5. The criteria distinguished the high-rated from the low-rated game. This is a heuristic evaluation model, not a player study.

**For Sporefall:** Directly usable as a design checklist. Two current failures map cleanly: weak floor-1 enemies violate Challenge (apathy), and kids not knowing what their build does violates Feedback ("immediate feedback on their actions"). The Control criterion about strategies the player chooses, not ones "planned by the game developers", argues against a single dominant build. Social Interaction covers the co-op.

## 5. Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi (2012) - challenge and enjoyment

**Citation:** Abuhamdeh, S., & Csikszentmihalyi, M. (2012). The importance of challenge for the enjoyment of intrinsically motivated, goal-directed activities. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38*(3), 317-330. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211427147 (Epub 8 Nov 2011; print March 2012)

**Opened:**
- https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=22067510&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PubMed PMID 22067510: journal, 2012 Mar, 38(3):317-30, both authors, abstract)
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146167211427147 (same metadata and abstract)

**Method:** Study 1: Internet chess (players' games against stronger vs weaker opponents, close vs lopsided games). Study 2: experience sampling across everyday activities. Sample sizes are not in the abstract; full text not opened (publisher paywall), so N is not reported here.

**Key quotes (abstract):**
- "The importance of challenge was evident at the subjective level (perceived challenge strongly predicted enjoyment) as well as the objective level (games against superior opponents were more enjoyable than games against inferior opponents, and close games were more enjoyable than blowouts)."
- "Activity motivation (intrinsically motivated, non-intrinsically motivated) and activity type (goal directed, non-goal directed) moderated the relation."

**Paraphrase:** In a competitive game played for its own sake, harder opponents and close outcomes were more enjoyable than easy wins. Challenge helps enjoyment mainly for activities that are intrinsically motivated and goal-directed, which a game run is.

**For Sporefall:** Floor-1 enemies that never threaten are the "blowout" case: predicted to be less enjoyable even though the player wins. Early floors can be easy to learn but should still push back. Caveat: adult chess players; no data on children.

## 6. Abuhamdeh, Csikszentmihalyi & Jalal (2015) - outcome uncertainty and suspense

**Citation:** Abuhamdeh, S., Csikszentmihalyi, M., & Jalal, B. (2015). Enjoying the possibility of defeat: Outcome uncertainty, suspense, and intrinsic motivation. *Motivation and Emotion, 39*(1), 1-10. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-014-9425-2 (online 3 Sep 2014; issue Feb 2015)

**Opened:**
- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-014-9425-2 (publisher page, reached via Springer cookie redirect: title, three authors, journal, vol 39, pp. 1-10, dates, DOI, abstract)
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/s11031-014-9425-2 (39(1), 1-10)
- https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/09/140903105443.htm (press release; secondary, source of the N and game below)

**Method:** Study 1 manipulated outcome uncertainty (score parity between player and opponent) in a competitive zero-sum video game; Study 2 added a behavioral measure (which game participants chose to replay). Per the ScienceDaily press release only (not the paper): 72 undergraduates played four rounds of Speed Slice on Nintendo Wii against a weak or a tough opponent, and "69 percent of the participants actually chose to play the game they won by a slim margin."

**Key quotes (abstract):**
- "Greater outcome uncertainty led to greater enjoyment, and this effect was mediated by suspense."
- "Although outperforming one's opponent by a wide margin maximized perceived competence, these games were less enjoyable than closer games with higher outcome uncertainty."
- "Participants chose to play games they previously rated as relatively high in suspense but relatively low in perceived competence over games which provided higher perceptions of competence but less suspense. Performance concern moderated this effect."

**Paraphrase:** Close fights are more fun than stomps, and suspense carries the effect. Winning big makes players feel most competent but they enjoy it less and choose it less. So felt competence and enjoyment can pull apart: players prefer an uncertain outcome to a guaranteed win.

**For Sporefall:** The dominant generic build that won every playtest fight removes outcome uncertainty; this paper predicts lower enjoyment and less desire to replay even though players feel strong. Boss fights where the right element (e.g. lightning) turns a coin-flip into a likely win, without guaranteeing it, keep suspense. Caveat: the moderator "performance concern" suggests some players (plausibly anxious kids) may react differently; the abstract does not give the direction.

## 7. Yee (2006) - motivations for play in online games

**Citation:** Yee, N. (2006). Motivations for play in online games. *CyberPsychology & Behavior, 9*(6), 772-775. https://doi.org/10.1089/cpb.2006.9.772

**Opened:**
- https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=17201605&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PubMed PMID 17201605: 2006 Dec, 9(6):772-5, Yee N, Stanford, abstract)
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1089/cpb.2006.9.772 (same metadata)
- https://eclass.uniwa.gr/modules/document/file.php/EEE195/2023-2024/Online%20gaming%20addiction/yee%202006.pdf (published version, "CYBERPSYCHOLOGY & BEHAVIOR Volume 9, Number 6, 2006", p. 772; a course-site copy of the publisher PDF)
- Also downloaded https://nickyee.com/pubs/Yee%20-%20Motivations%20(2007).pdf (author's own preprint; not used for quotes)

**Method:** 40-item motivation inventory (built from Bartle's types and earlier player surveys), online survey of 3,000 MMORPG players (EverQuest, Dark Age of Camelot, Ultima Online, Star Wars Galaxies). Principal components analysis: 10 subcomponents (60% of variance), then a second PCA grouped them into 3 main components (55% of variance). Oblique (Promax) rotation.

**Model (Table 1):**
- Achievement: Advancement, Mechanics, Competition
- Social: Socializing, Relationship, Teamwork
- Immersion: Discovery, Role-Playing, Customization, Escapism

**Key quotes:**
- Quote (abstract): "The analysis revealed 10 motivation subcomponents that grouped into three overarching components (achievement, social, and immersion)."
- Quote (Mechanics subcomponent definition): "Having an interest in analyzing the underlying rules and system in order to optimize character performance" (Table 1 keywords: "Numbers, Optimization, Templating, Analysis")
- Quote (Teamwork): "Deriving satisfaction from being part of a group effort."
- Quote (vs. Bartle): "play motivations in MMORPGs do not suppress each other as Bartle suggested. If a player scored high on the achievement component that did not mean they scored low on the social component."
- Quote: "the variation in the achievement component is in fact better explained by age than gender."

**Paraphrase:** Player motives are separate, weakly correlated dimensions (r < 0.10 between the three main components), not mutually exclusive types. "Mechanics" is the motive to understand and optimize the system.

**For Sporefall:** The combinatorial mod system serves the Mechanics motive (theorycrafting an ordered build) and Discovery (finding combos). Co-op serves Teamwork. Because motives do not trade off, one kid can enjoy optimizing while another enjoys the group effort. Caveat: MMORPG players (the paper cites prior work putting the average MMORPG player at 26 years old), not children or short arcade runs.

## 8. Tondello, Arrambide, Ribeiro, Cen & Nacke (2019) - player traits model

**Citation:** Tondello, G. F., Arrambide, K., Ribeiro, G., Cen, A. J., & Nacke, L. E. (2019). "I don't fit into a single type": A trait model and scale of game playing preferences. In D. Lamas, F. Loizides, L. Nacke, H. Petrie, M. Winckler, & P. Zaphiris (Eds.), *Human-Computer Interaction - INTERACT 2019* (Lecture Notes in Computer Science, pp. 375-395). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-29384-0_23 (LNCS volume number not shown on the pages I opened)

**Opened:**
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-030-29384-0_23 (title, all five authors, 2019, LNCS / INTERACT 2019, pp. 375-395)
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-030-29384-0 (proceedings editors, ISBNs 9783030293833 / 9783030293840)
- https://www.gamefulbits.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/2019-A-Trait-Model-and-Scale-of-Game-Playing-Preferences.pdf (authors' pre-print, marked "17th IFIP TC.13 International Conference on Human-Computer Interaction INTERACT 2019 (pre-print)"; quotes below are from this pre-print)

**Method:** online survey, 10 candidate items per trait; 332 valid responses (from 350; ages 15-57, M = 25.7), recruited via social media and mailing lists. Exploratory factor analysis to keep 5 items per trait, then confirmatory factor analysis (SEM); test-retest subsample (70 participants, r = .76 to .91). Final 25-item scale.

**Five traits:** aesthetic, narrative, goal, social, challenge orientation. Two were renamed after the data: "immersion" became narrative orientation and "action" became challenge orientation.

**Key quotes:**
- Quote (abstract): "personality research has lately favoured trait theories, which recognize that people's preferences are composed of a sum of different characteristics."
- Quote (challenge orientation): "players who score high on this trait generally prefer difficult games and hard challenges. On the other hand, players who score low prefer easier or casual games."
- Quote (on action vs challenge): "in our study the challenge-oriented motivations were more pronounced than action-oriented ones."
- Quote (on Yee's Quantic Foundry profile): "This gamer motivation profile could be used to aid in the design of personalized games; however, its survey is a proprietary instrument, which makes it difficult to apply in every situation, especially for smaller game studios."
- Quote (on SDT scales): "SDT alone cannot be used to understand player preferences."

**Paraphrase:** Players are mixtures of traits, not single types. A validated, free 25-item scale measures five preference traits. The authors map their traits onto Yee's gamer motivation profile (challenge to mastery, goal to achievement, aesthetic to creativity, narrative to immersion, social to social) but say the profile's survey is proprietary.

**For Sporefall:** The kids will differ in challenge orientation; one may want hard bosses, another may want collection/goal content (collecting mods). Build systems can serve goal-oriented players (collect all mods) and challenge-oriented ones (beat the boss with the counter-element) at once. Caveat: minimum age 15, so no evidence on 8-12 year olds.

## 9. Quantic Foundry Gamer Motivation Model (Yee & Ducheneaut) - industry model

**Verdict: industry / market-research model, not a peer-reviewed validated instrument.** I found no peer-reviewed paper that reports the model's development or psychometric validation. What exists: company web pages and PDFs, a one-page CHI PLAY 2016 item by Yee, an edited-book chapter (OUP 2018), and third-party academic uses. Its academic ancestor (Yee 2006, entry 7; Yee, Ducheneaut & Nelson 2012 CHI) is peer-reviewed. Tondello et al. 2019 (entry 8, peer-reviewed) cite the model only via Quantic Foundry blog posts and call its survey "a proprietary instrument".

**Primary (company) sources opened:**
- https://quanticfoundry.com/gamer-motivation-model/ (fetched via curl; page by Nick Yee). Quote: "Using a factor analysis on data collected from over 140,000 gamers, we identified how these motivations clustered together." Quote: "Quantic Foundry is a market research company focused on gamer motivation."
- https://quanticfoundry.com/2015/07/20/how-we-developed-the-gamer-motivation-profile-v2/ (Nick Yee, 20 July 2015). Quote: "We validated and refined our motivations model based on data from 30,000 gamers around the world." Method stated there: factor analysis run separately on US + Canada, Indonesia and EU samples ("The number of factors that emerged and the factor composition across these 3 regions were identical"), then hierarchical clustering into 6 pairs and 3 high-level groups.
- https://quanticfoundry.com/2015/12/15/handy-reference/ (Nick Yee, 15 Dec 2015). Quote: "A total of over 140,000 gamers worldwide have now completed the Gamer Motivation Profile."
- https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Gamer-Motivation-Model-Reference-v3.pdf (reference sheets v3). Quote: "Using data from 2M+ gamers, we identified 12 distinct gaming motivations. These motivations group in 6 related pairs." **Model (from the v3 reference PDF), 12 motivations in 6 pairs, 3 clusters:**
- Action-Social cluster: Action (Destruction, Excitement); Social (Competition, Community)
- Mastery-Achievement cluster: Mastery (Challenge, Strategy); Achievement (Completion, Power)
- Immersion-Creativity cluster: Immersion (Fantasy, Story); Creativity (Design, Discovery)
- Selected definitions from the 2015 development post (quotes): "Challenge: The preference for games of skill and enjoyment of overcoming difficult challenges." "Strategy: The enjoyment of games that require careful decision-making and strategic thinking." "Discovery: The desire to explore, tinker, and experiment with the game world." "Community: The enjoyment of interacting and collaborating with other players."

## 10. Chen (2007) - Flow in games (optional)

**Citation:** Chen, J. (2007). Flow in games (and everything else). *Communications of the ACM, 50*(4), 31-34. https://doi.org/10.1145/1232743.1232769

**Opened:**
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/1232743.1232769 (title, author, April 2007, CACM 50(4), 31-34)
- https://www.jenovachen.com/flowingames/p31-chen.pdf (author-hosted copy of the published pages; footer "COMMUNICATIONS OF THE ACM April 2007/Vol. 50, No. 4", section label "Viewpoint")

**Type:** Viewpoint (opinion essay), no empirical data.

**Key quotes:**
- "Most games offer only a single narrow, static experience (the red line in Figure 2). It might keep the typical player in the Flow but will not be fun for the hardcore or even the novice player."
- "Too many choices overwhelm the user and maybe even the computer." [PDF text reads "T oo" from kerning]
- "The best way for game designers to avoid these counterproductive situations is to embed the player's choices into the core activities of the interactive experience."
- Four-step conclusion (bullets removed): "Mix and match the components of Flow; Keep the user's experience within the user's Flow Zone; Offer adaptive choices, allowing different users to enjoy the Flow in their own way; and Embed choices inside the core activities to ensure the Flow is never interrupted."

**Paraphrase:** Players differ in skill, so one fixed difficulty curve misses novices and experts. Rather than a difficulty menu, let players pick their own challenge through choices made inside play, and avoid choice overload that breaks concentration.

**For Sporefall:** A father and 8-12 year olds have different flow zones in the same session. Build choice is a way to let each set their own challenge in play. Chen's warning on choice overload supports keeping mod picks few and fast, taken in the flow of play, not in long menus. Opinion piece: cite as design argument, not evidence.

## 11. Children and flow / SDT (optional)

### 11a. Inal & Cagiltay (2007) - flow in children aged 7-9

**Citation:** Inal, Y., & Cagiltay, K. (2007). Flow experiences of children in an interactive social game environment. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 38*(3), 455-464. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2007.00709.x

**Opened:**
- https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2007.00709.x (title, both authors, 2007, BJET 38(3), 455-464, abstract)
- https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1111/j.1467-8535.2007.00709.x?fields=title,authors,year,venue,abstract (same abstract)

**Method (abstract):** 33 children aged 7 to 9, observed for 6 weeks; observations and interviews; flow-scale items given orally in interviews. Small, qualitative-leaning sample.

**Key quotes (abstract):**
- "Results revealed that flow experiences occur more among boys than girls during gameplay."
- "Challenge and complexity elements of games had more effect on the flow experiences of the children than clear feedback."

**Paraphrase:** Even 7-9 year olds reported flow driven mainly by challenge and complexity; clear feedback mattered less for flow. Boys responded more to gameplay mechanics (ludology), girls more to narrative.

**For Sporefall:** The closest evidence to the target age: young kids do respond to real challenge and to mechanical complexity, which argues against making floor 1 toothless. The weaker role of feedback is about flow, not about understanding a build; it does not remove the readability problem. Small N, one game environment, observation plus interview.

### 11b. Noon, Carbone, Swinnen & Vandenbosch (2024) - adolescents, SDT, longitudinal

**Citation:** Noon, E. J., Carbone, L., Swinnen, B., & Vandenbosch, L. (2024). Adolescents' videogame play, need satisfaction, and self-esteem: A self-determination perspective. *Current Psychology, 43*(24), 21192-21207. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-024-05933-6

**For Sporefall:** Mostly background. It is about well-being, not enjoyment design, and the sample is older (mean age 15) than the 8-12 target.

## Cross-cutting takeaways for Sporefall (from the verified sources above)

- **Dominant build = autonomy + suspense failure.** Autonomy needs real choice over strategy (Ryan et al. 2006; GameFlow Control criterion). A guaranteed win lowers enjoyment and replay choice even while raising felt competence (Abuhamdeh et al. 2015).
- **Toothless floor 1 = challenge failure.** Close games beat blowouts (Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi 2012); challenge below skill gives apathy (Sweetser & Wyeth 2005). Children aged 7-9 in one small study reported flow driven mostly by challenge and complexity (Inal & Cagiltay 2007).
- **Unreadable builds = feedback/competence failure.** Competence depends on informative feedback (Ryan et al. 2006; Przybylski et al. 2010); GameFlow requires immediate feedback on actions and knowing one's status.
- **Controls are a gate, not the fun.** Mastery of controls is necessary but not sufficient (Przybylski et al. 2010); intuitive controls act through competence and autonomy (Ryan et al. 2006).
- **Mixed motives in one household.** Motives are independent traits, not exclusive types (Yee 2006; Tondello et al. 2019). Adapt through choices made inside play, and avoid choice overload (Chen 2007, opinion).
- **Age gap in evidence.** Almost all of this evidence comes from adults or adolescents. The only child sample here is Inal & Cagiltay (N = 33, ages 7-9).
