## Juul (2002), The Open and the Closed

- **Citation:** Juul, Jesper (2002). "The Open and the Closed: Games of Emergence and Games of Progression." In F. Mäyrä (Ed.), *Computer Games and Digital Cultures Conference Proceedings*, pp. 323-329. Tampere: Tampere University Press. DOI: 10.26503/dl.v2002i1.9 (DiGRA Digital Library).
- **URLs opened:** https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html (full text; its header gives authors, venue, editor, pages, publisher, year). https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/9 (confirms title, author, 2002, proceedings, DOI).
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE: "two basic game structures, that of emergence (a number of simple rules combining to form interesting variation) and that of progression (separate challenges presented serially)."
  - QUOTE: "Emergence is the primordial game structure, where a game is specified as a small number of rules that combine and yield large numbers of game variations, which the players then design strategies for dealing with. [...] Emergence games tend to be replayable and tend to foster tournaments and strategy guides."
  - QUOTE: "In progression games, the player has to perform a predefined set of actions in order to complete the game."
  - QUOTE: "If the game allows for a good strategy that leads to interesting interaction, it is a good game. If the optimal strategy for playing the game leads to dull game sessions, the game will be considered dull."
  - Juul lists "Dominant, complete strategies. (Completely defined strategies that will always lead to victory.)" as one level of emergent property.
  - Paraphrase: variation should come from a few interacting rules, not enumerated content. Players pursue the goal and so converge on whatever strategy the rules favour; the game is judged by the play that optimal strategy produces.
- **For Sporefall:** Direct support for per-mod transformation rules over a hand-authored pair table: a small rule set that combines is Juul's definition of emergence. His "optimal strategy leads to dull sessions" line gives a scholarly basis for the owner's rule that a dominant build is a failure even when fun: kids will converge on frost if it is optimal, and every run then plays the same.

  - QUOTE (Koster 2012 blog, restating the theory): "fun is the feeling you get when you are exercising your brain by solving a cognitive puzzle. [...] your brain is basically trying to perceive a pattern; victory usually comes from identifying the pattern, then correctly executing on some action that the pattern does not account for."

- **For Sporefall:** A universal answer (frost, then fire) is a mastered pattern: once the kids learn "frost beats everything", every new enemy "can be done using the same tactics," which is Koster's definition of added content that adds no challenge. The census tool can operationalise his test: if a new arena or enemy leaves the top build unchanged, it added no new pattern to learn.

## Paul (2011), Optimizing Play

- **Citation:** Paul, Christopher A. (2011). "Optimizing Play: How Theorycraft Changes Gameplay and Design." *Game Studies* 11(2), May 2011. ISSN 1604-7982. Open access, no DOI or page numbers shown.
- **URL opened (confirmed metadata and full text):** https://gamestudies.org/1102/articles/paul (header: volume 11, issue 2, May 2011; author, Seattle University).
- **Method:** rhetorical analysis of player discourse about World of Warcraft theorycraft (forums, blogs, WoW Insider comments). Not empirical/quantitative; no sample size.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (definition): "Theorycraft, a strategy designed around the mathematical analysis of WoW, is a discursive construct predicated on advising players how to optimally 'play' WoW, suggesting what equipment to wear, what talents to choose, and an order in which to cast spells."
  - QUOTE: "the goal of theorycrafting is to figure out what is best and, once the best is determined, the choices are distilled to the single best choice. The moment 'total' understanding is derived, or the perception of total understanding exists, there is only one choice for 'good' players to make and, consequently, a host of problems are created as players see many options that are not 'best' as broken."
  - QUOTE: "Theorycraft changes how play occurs, shifting it from sifting through a sea of choices to a search for the optimal solution, resulting in what are often called 'cookie cutter builds.'"
  - QUOTE: "Lacking other modes of analysis the game loses depth, variety, and choice, as one approach marginalizes all others."
  - Paraphrase: a large nominal choice space collapses to one choice once players (or a spreadsheet) find the best build; the rest then reads as broken. Designers respond (e.g. WoW added training dummies so players could test builds).
- **For Sporefall:** The census is in-house theorycraft: it finds the single best build before players do. Paul's account of how a big choice space collapses into "cookie cutter builds" is the scholarly case for the owner's rule that a dominant build fails even when fun. It is also a warning that kids who find "frost wins" will read every other mod as broken. Note "order in which to cast spells" is part of his definition, which maps to Sporefall's ordered mod sequence.

## Salen & Zimmerman (2004), Rules of Play

- **Citation:** Salen, Katie, & Zimmerman, Eric (2004). *Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. ISBN 0-262-24045-9. Copyright page reads "© 2004 Massachusetts Institute of Technology"; LC CIP number 2003045923. Open Library lists release as October 1, 2003, 688 pp. Cite as 2004 (the copyright year).
- **URLs opened:** https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/aujimtec3175fall2021/files/2017/08/1-rules-of-play-game-design-fundamentals.pdf (full-text PDF posted on a CUNY course site; the copyright/CIP page confirms title, authors, MIT Press, 2004, ISBN). https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0262240459.json (title, subtitle, MIT Press, authors resolve to Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman). This PDF is an ebook rendering with chapter-relative page numbers, so **I could not confirm printed page numbers**. The page numbers usually cited for the meaningful-play definition come from secondary sources; check them against a print copy.
- **Key claims (verbatim from the PDF):**
  - QUOTE (Ch. 3, descriptive definition): "Meaningful play in a game emerges from the relationship between player action and system outcome; it is the process by which a player takes action within the designed system of a game and the system responds to the action. The meaning of an action in a game resides in the relationship between action and outcome."
  - QUOTE (Ch. 3, evaluative definition): "Meaningful play occurs when the relationships between actions and outcomes in a game are both discernable and integrated into the larger context of the game. Creating meaningful play is the goal of successful game design." (The chapter summary words it as "Meaningful play is what occurs when...".)
  - QUOTE (Ch. 3 summary): "Discernability means that a player can perceive the immediate outcome of an action. Integration means that the outcome of an action is woven into the game system as a whole."
  - QUOTE (Ch. 14, Games as Emergent Systems): "Coupled interactions help produce global patterns across a system; context-dependent interactions ensure that the exact arrangement of these patterns are dynamically changing over time." And: "In games, emergence arises through the interaction of the formal game system and decisions made by players."
  - QUOTE (Ch. 19, Games as Game Theory Systems): "A degenerate strategy is a way of playing a game that ensures victory every time. [...] Degenerate strategies can be painful for game designers, as players shortcut all of the attention lavished on a game's rich set of possibilities. Try to find degenerate strategies and get rid of them! [...] A close analysis of your game design can sometimes reveal them but the only real way to root them out is through rigorous playtesting. If you see players drawn to a particular set of strategies again and again, they may be exploiting a weakness in your design."
  - QUOTE (Ch. 21, Breaking the Rules): "Degenerate strategies often appear in complex games, where the numerous permutations of play sometimes afford shortcuts in the space of possibility." They cite early Magic: The Gathering card combinations "simply too powerful" that were banned.
- **For Sporefall:**
  - Meaningful play sets two tests for each mod. Discernable: a kid can see what frost-then-pierce did. Integrated: the choice changes later encounters, not just damage numbers.
  - The degenerate-strategy passages back the owner's ruling, and they endorse the census: "rigorous playtesting" plus watching for "a particular set of strategies again and again" is exactly what the census measures.
  - Ch. 14's "coupled, context-dependent interactions" supports a grammar (a mod's effect depends on its neighbours in the sequence) over a flat pair table.

## Sweetser & Wiles (2005), Scripting versus Emergence

- **Citation:** Sweetser, Penelope, & Wiles, Janet (2005). "Scripting versus Emergence: Issues for Game Developers and Players in Game Environment Design." *International Journal of Intelligent Games and Simulation* 4(1), 1-9. ISSN 1477-2043. No DOI found.
- **URLs opened:** https://eprints.qut.edu.au/46349/ (QUT ePrints record via WebFetch: title, authors, 2005, IJIGS 4(1) pp. 1-9, ISSN; curl was blocked). Full text: https://web.archive.org/web/2015/http://www.emergenceingames.com/web_images/Sweetser_IJIGS.pdf (10 pages, header "© IJIGS/University of Wolverhampton/EUROSIS", authors at University of Queensland; abstract matches the ePrints record). Semantic Scholar search confirms title, authors, 2005.
- **Type:** conceptual/position paper; no empirical study.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract): "The first, referred to as scripting, requires developers to anticipate, hand-craft and script specific game objects, events and player interactions. The second, known as emergence, involves defining general, global rules that interact to give rise to emergent gameplay. [...] It is concluded that scripting and emergence are two extremes of the same continuum, neither of which are ideal for game development."
  - QUOTE: "design general, rule-based systems that allow the creation of gameplay out of combinations of existing game elements with globally defined, consistent characteristics and behaviour."
  - QUOTE (costs of emergence): "Considerations of an emergent approach for game developers include significant planning and tuning in development, a loss of creative control, difficulties in giving feedback and direction to players and uncertainty in how the game will respond to the player. However, emergent systems are easier to modify and extend".
  - QUOTE: "Emergent games also have high replayability as each time the player plays the game they make different decisions".
- **For Sporefall:** A per-mod grammar is the "general, rule-based" end of this continuum; a hand-authored pair table is the scripted end. The paper names the cost: "uncertainty in how the game will respond". That cost is the argument for the census, which turns the grammar's unknown outputs into measured ones.

## Jaffe et al. (2012), Evaluating Competitive Game Balance with Restricted Play

- **Citation:** Jaffe, Alexander, Miller, Alex, Andersen, Erik, Liu, Yun-En, Karlin, Anna, & Popović, Zoran (2012). "Evaluating Competitive Game Balance with Restricted Play." *Proceedings of the Eighth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Interactive Digital Entertainment (AIIDE)* 8(1), 26-31. DOI: 10.1609/aiide.v8i1.12513
- **URLs opened:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1609/aiide.v8i1.12513 (confirmed all six authors, 2012, AIIDE proceedings 8(1), pp. 26-31, AAAI). Full text: https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~zoran/jaffe2012ecg.pdf (University of Washington; footer "Proceedings, The Eighth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Interactive Digital Entertainment", p. 26). OJS page: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12513 (found in search, not opened).
- **Method:** formal framework plus a prototype tool that computes win rates of optimal agents under behaviour restrictions for a family of two-player, zero-sum, perfect-information games. Case study: iterative balancing of an educational card game, *Monsters Divided*. Optimal agents, not human players.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract): "We argue for a formulation in which carefully restricted agents are played against standard agents. We develop this restricted-play balance framework, and evaluate its utility by building a tool capable of calculating measures of balance for a large family of games."
  - QUOTE: "a designer could ask whether one action is more powerful than the others. To do so, we may measure the win rate of an agent who is restricted from ever (or often) taking that action, against an agent who exploits this restriction."
  - QUOTE: "The difficulty of balancing arises from the emergent complexity of systems. Small tweaks can have unexpected consequences for a game's viable strategies [...] While developing Halo 3, Bungie discovered that the sniper rifle was overpowered, subsuming several interesting strategies".
  - QUOTE: "first-person shooters typically allow players to play according to their preference of weapon and play-style: no one weapon should be essential."
  - QUOTE (case study): "restricting the player from playing Green has a smaller impact on the win-rate than restricting Red or Blue. This suggests Green as under-powered."
  - They frame the tool as an "'early warning system', that illuminates certain flagrant imbalances upfront, saving playtesting time".
- **For Sporefall:** The clearest template for turning the census into a dominance metric: run the fixed bot with each mod banned (e.g. "no frost") and measure the drop in clear rate. A mod whose removal costs the most is essential, which violates Jaffe's "no one weapon should be essential" goal for shooters. A mod whose removal costs nothing is dead weight. Caveat: Jaffe is two-player zero-sum with optimal agents; Sporefall is co-op PvE with a fixed heuristic bot, so the method transfers but the guarantees do not.

## Zook, Fruchter & Riedl (2014), Automatic Playtesting via Active Learning

- **Citation:** Zook, Alexander, Fruchter, Eric, & Riedl, Mark O. (2014). "Automatic Playtesting for Game Parameter Tuning via Active Learning." In M. Mateas, T. Barnes, & I. Bogost (Eds.), *Proceedings of the 9th International Conference on the Foundations of Digital Games (FDG 2014)*, Liberty of the Seas, Caribbean, April 3-7, 2014. Society for the Advancement of the Science of Digital Games. Also arXiv:1908.01417 (posted 2019). No page numbers or DOI confirmed.
- **URLs opened:** http://www.fdg2014.org/papers/fdg2014_paper_39.pdf (full text on the FDG 2014 site; authors at Georgia Tech). https://researchr.org/publication/fdg-2014 (lists the paper and authors in the FDG 2014 table of contents; editors and publisher). http://export.arxiv.org/api/query?id_list=1908.01417 (title, authors, abstract).
- **Method and sample:** active learning (Gaussian processes, kernel SVMs, and neuro-evolved neural networks as design models, with several acquisition functions) chooses which parameter settings to playtest next. Case study: a shoot-'em-up with two objectives, tuning enemy parameters (regression) and control settings (preference). Online deployment, unpaid volunteers. QUOTE: "For our regression experiment this resulted in data from 138 players and 991 waves of the game total [...] For our preference experiment we had 57 players, 47 of these provided only binary responses [...] to yield 416 paired comparisons." Evaluated with 10-fold cross-validation.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract): "We show how active learning techniques can formalize and automate a subset of playtesting goals. Specifically, we focus on the low-level parameter tuning required to balance a game once the mechanics have been chosen."
  - QUOTE: "Overall our results show AL is a promising approach for reducing the number of playtests needed to achieve a design goal. [...] No single acquisition function, objective function, or acquisition-objective function pair was optimal across cases and number of playtests."
- **For Sporefall:** The census brute-forces every build against every arena across seeds. As the mod count grows the build space explodes; active learning is the published way to choose which builds and parameter values to simulate next. Zook scopes it to tuning "once the mechanics have been chosen", which fits tuning grammar coefficients (frost slow strength, fire DoT) more than choosing the grammar itself.

## Holmgård, Green, Liapis & Togelius (2019), Automated Playtesting with Procedural Personas

- **Citation:** Holmgård, Christoffer, Green, Michael Cerny, Liapis, Antonios, & Togelius, Julian (2019). "Automated Playtesting With Procedural Personas Through MCTS With Evolved Heuristics." *IEEE Transactions on Games* 11(4), 352-362. DOI: 10.1109/TG.2018.2808198. Preprint arXiv:1802.06881 (2018). Note: some reference managers list 2018, the early-access year; Crossref gives the issue date December 2019.
- **URLs opened:** Crossref query api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=... (returned DOI 10.1109/tg.2018.2808198, all four authors, IEEE Transactions on Games 11(4) 352-362, 2019-12). http://export.arxiv.org/api/query?id_list=1802.06881 and full text https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.06881.
- **Method:** MCTS agents whose node-selection formula (in place of UCB1) is evolved with genetic programming, one per persona utility function, run on levels of *MiniDungeons 2* ("a deterministic, turn-based rogue-like"). Agents only, no human participants in this paper.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract): "procedural personas are implemented using a variation of Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) where the node selection criteria are developed using evolutionary computation, replacing the standard UCB1 criterion of MCTS. [...] In short, we use artificially intelligent personas to construct synthetic playtesters."
  - QUOTE (personas): "Runner aims to reach the exit. / Monster Killer wants to kill monsters. / Treasure Collector desires to collect treasure. / Completionist attempts to consume any game object that can be collected or killed". They were "defined based on our design experience and intuition".
  - QUOTE: "these four single-minded personas capture a large part of the potential play space in MiniDungeons 2."
- **For Sporefall:** The census uses one fixed bot. Holmgård's result argues for several bots with different utility functions (e.g. "rusher", "kiter", "collector"), because a build that dominates under one play style may not under another. That tests whether frost is universal or only universal for the census bot. The team's LLM persona playtesters are a language-model analogue of the same idea.

## Hernandez, Gbadamosi, Goodman & Walker (2020), Metagame Autobalancing

- **Citation:** Hernandez, Daniel, Toyin Gbadamosi, Charles Takashi, Goodman, James, & Walker, James Alfred (2020). "Metagame Autobalancing for Competitive Multiplayer Games." *2020 IEEE Conference on Games (CoG)*, pp. 275-282. DOI: 10.1109/CoG47356.2020.9231762. Preprint arXiv:2006.04419.
- **URLs opened:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/CoG47356.2020.9231762 (authors, 2020, IEEE CoG, pp. 275-282). Full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.04419 (University of York; Queen Mary University of London).
- **Method:** the designer draws a target "response graph" over high-level strategies (which should beat which, and by how much). Simulated agents play each strategy and yield an empirical win-rate matrix; Bayesian optimisation (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator, via Optuna) searches game parameters to minimise the distance between the empirical and target graphs. Demonstrated on Rock-Paper-Scissors variants and an asymmetric fighting game. Simulation only.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract): "Our approach requires a designer to construct an intuitive graphical representation of their meta-game target, representing the relative scores that high-level strategies (or decks, or character types) should experience. This permits more sophisticated balance targets to be defined beyond a simple requirement of equal win chances."
  - QUOTE: "Changes to individual game elements or rules can have an impact on the balance between high-level strategies that depend on these, but this impact is unknown before changes are made [...] We term this balance between emergent high-level strategies the 'Meta-game balance'."
  - QUOTE: "A game designer may want to ensure that no one deck type dominates".
  - QUOTE: "In an RPG each node of the response graph might represent a character class; Paladin, Wizard, Sniper etc., as we seek to balance these against each other. At a lower level, each node might represent an individual weapon." They note the cost of computing the matrix grows "exponentially with respect to the number of agents."
- **For Sporefall:** A way to state "no dominant build" as a target rather than "equal win rates". Sporefall is PvE, so the graph's nodes are builds and arena archetypes, and the target is an intransitive, rock-paper-scissors-like pattern: frost wins the swarm arena, fire wins the armoured arena, and nothing wins all of them. The census already produces the build-by-arena matrix; this paper supplies the objective and the optimiser for tuning grammar coefficients toward it.

## de Mesentier Silva et al. (2019), Evolving the Hearthstone Meta

- **Citation:** de Mesentier Silva, Fernando, Canaan, Rodrigo, Lee, Scott, Fontaine, Matthew C., Togelius, Julian, & Hoover, Amy K. (2019). "Evolving the Hearthstone Meta." *2019 IEEE Conference on Games (CoG)*, pp. 1-8. DOI: 10.1109/CIG.2019.8847966. Preprint arXiv:1907.01623.
- **URLs opened:** Crossref query (api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=...) returned DOI, all six authors, IEEE CoG 2019, pp. 1-8. Full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.01623 (arXiv ID confirmed via export.arxiv.org API title search).
- **Method:** simulated Hearthstone matches between fixed decks played by AI agents. The build-level signal is the deck-vs-deck win-rate matrix. Three experiments: (1) evolutionary search for card changes that bring every matchup near 50%; (2) multi-objective search that also minimises the number of changes; (3) nerfing single cards, ranked by win rate when drawn or played.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE: "in competitive games where players are motivated to win, players often focus on gathering particular combinations of objects that maximize their perceived chances of winning. [...] In an unbalanced game, there may be a single degenerate strategy that is unbeatable or it may be impossible to play a particular character class well. Such a game is essentially broken and experienced players tend to quickly lose interest."
  - QUOTE: "Even a single misbalanced card could result in large disruptions to the metagame [...] A balanced metagame promotes a variety of competitive decks."
  - QUOTE: "We observe that nerfing cards that originally had a higher win rate when drawn (and to a lesser extent, win rate when played) tends to impact the deck's win rate more, suggesting that this could be a reasonable metric to guide the search for effective balance changes".
- **For Sporefall:** Closest published analogue to the census: builds made of combinable parts, scored by a simulated win-rate matrix, then tuned by search. Two direct transfers. (1) A per-mod "win rate when present" statistic from census runs flags which mod to nerf first (it would have flagged frost). (2) The minimise-changes objective suits a game kids already know: fix frost with the smallest rule change, not a reshuffle.

## Fontaine et al. (2019), Mapping Hearthstone Deck Spaces through MAP-Elites with Sliding Boundaries

- **Citation:** Fontaine, Matthew C., Lee, Scott, Soros, L. B., De Mesentier Silva, Fernando, Togelius, Julian, & Hoover, Amy K. (2019). "Mapping Hearthstone Deck Spaces through MAP-Elites with Sliding Boundaries." *Proceedings of the Genetic and Evolutionary Computation Conference (GECCO '19)*, Prague, pp. 161-169. ACM. DOI: 10.1145/3321707.3321794. Preprint arXiv:1904.10656.
- **URLs opened:** Crossref query returned DOI, authors, GECCO 2019, pp. 161-169, ACM. Full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.10656 (header "GECCO '19, July 13-17, 2019, Prague, Czech Republic").
- **Method:** quality-diversity search (MAP-Elites with cell boundaries that slide to follow the population's distribution) evolves Hearthstone decks. Each deck is scored by simulated play and binned by a behaviour vector (average mana cost of the 30 cards and variance of mana costs), with aggro and control play-style heuristics. Frequent-pattern (Apriori) mining of the elite decks finds cards that recur across niches; those cards are nerfed and the search is rerun.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract): "We argue that these algorithms are perfectly suited to the rich domain of video games, which contains many relevant problems with a multitude of successful strategies and often also multiple dimensions along which solutions can vary. [...] Results suggest MESB finds diverse ways of playing the game well along the selected behavioral dimensions."
  - QUOTE: "Potentially overpowered cards and card combinations were identfied with an Apriori analysis of evolved decks; the diversity assured by MESB implies that if a pattern is discovered in multiple decks, it is almost certainly powerful in a variety of settings (perhaps too powerful)." (sic, "identfied")
- **For Sporefall:** A sharper dominance detector than "best build overall". Bin the census builds by behaviour (range, crowd control vs single-target, element), keep the best build in each bin, and flag any mod or mod pair that appears in the elite of most bins. Frost showing up across unrelated niches is exactly their "powerful in a variety of settings (perhaps too powerful)" signal. It also gives a healthy-state target: many bins, each with a different elite.

## Mahlmann, Togelius & Yannakakis (2012), Evolving Card Sets Towards Balancing Dominion

- **Citation:** Mahlmann, Tobias, Togelius, Julian, & Yannakakis, Georgios N. (2012). "Evolving Card Sets Towards Balancing Dominion." *2012 IEEE Congress on Evolutionary Computation (CEC)*, WCCI 2012, Brisbane, June 10-15, 2012, pp. 1-8. DOI: 10.1109/CEC.2012.6256441
- **URLs opened:** https://zenodo.org/api/records/1266109 (title, three authors, 2012-06-01, DOI, abstract). - **Method:** evolutionary search over 10-card Dominion kingdoms. Fitness functions are built from simulated play by three AI agents of different skill (e.g. how far the stronger agent wins by). ## Bateni, Pratt & Whitehead (2025 preprint), Rule Synergy Analysis using LLMs (NOT peer-reviewed)

- **Citation:** Bateni, Bahar, Pratt, Benjamin, & Whitehead, Jim (2025). "Rule Synergy Analysis using LLMs: State of the Art and Implications." arXiv:2508.19484. arXiv comment: "Submitted for publication at the IEEE Transactions on Games 2024, Special Issue on Large Language Models and Games." **Preprint.** Crossref showed no published version under this title. The same authors' peer-reviewed AIIDE 2025 paper (DOI 10.1609/aiide.v21i1.36804, "LLM Game Rule Understanding Through Out-of-Distribution Fine-Tuning", AIIDE 21(1), 2-11) is a different study on Solitaire rule understanding.
- **URLs opened:** http://export.arxiv.org/api/query?id_list=2508.19484 (title, authors, date, comment); full text https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19484. Crossref query for the published-version check.
- **Method:** a hand-labelled dataset of every ordered pair of 75 Slay the Spire cards (5,625 pairs), each labelled positive, negative or no synergy for "card A first and card B after". LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, among others) classify the pairs from card text alone; the main metric is F1.
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (definition): "A synergy effect is defined as the interaction of two or more elements to produce a combined effect greater than the sum of their individual effects."
  - QUOTE: "A synergy between card A and card B is defined as whether playing card A first and card B after results in an effect that differs from the sum of their individual effects."
  - Dataset counts (Table I): positive synergy 947 (16.8%), no synergy 4553 (81%), negative synergy 125 (2.2%).
  - QUOTE (abstract): "while LLMs excel at identifying non-synergistic pairs, they struggle with detecting positive and, particularly, negative synergies. We categorize common error types, including issues with timing, defining game states, and following game rules."
- **For Sporefall:** Three uses, with the preprint caveat. (1) An ordered-pair synergy definition that matches a sequenced wand: A-then-B differs from B-then-A. (2) Evidence of the cost of a pair table: 75 parts already needed 5,625 hand labels, and even a commercially tuned set had synergies in under 20% of pairs. (3) A warning for the LLM persona playtesters: models reading mod descriptions will under-detect synergies, especially anti-synergies and order effects. Use the census (dynamic evaluation) as ground truth, not LLM judgement from text.

## Grow, Dickinson, Pagnutti, Wardrip-Fruin & Mateas (2017), Crafting in Games

- **Citation:** Grow, April, Dickinson, Melanie, Pagnutti, Johnathan, Wardrip-Fruin, Noah, & Mateas, Michael (2017). "Crafting in Games." *DHQ: Digital Humanities Quarterly* 11(4). Open access; article number 000339. No DOI or page numbers shown.
- **URL opened:** https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000339.pdf (header: "DHQ: Digital Humanities Quarterly 2017 Volume 11 Number 4", title, five authors, all University of California Santa Cruz).
- **Method:** design-space analysis. Seven dimensions applied to "64 crafting systems across 47 example games" (from the abstract).
- **Key claims:**
  - QUOTE (abstract, dimensions): "recipe definition (how well-defined are the player steps for creating a virtual object), [...] variable outcome (how much can the result vary when the player performs the same crafting actions), system recognition of outcome (to what degree do other game systems 'understand' and take account of what a player has made), player expressiveness (how big is the creative space provided to the player), and progression".
  - QUOTE: "At one extreme is the strongly defined recipe which dictates recipe ingredients precisely [...] This provides less control for the player, but greater control to the game creator, who can explicitly enumerate all possible craftable objects. At the other extreme are crafting systems with undefined recipes. Such systems use an underlying simulation to determine what happens when objects are combined to create a new object. By not enforcing specific ingredient combinations, this opens more possibilities for experimental and creative play, but reduces the game creators' control over crafting."
  - QUOTE: "When players discover these implicit recipes, without having looked them up or known about them beforehand, this style of system recognition can be a delightful and surprising meeting of minds between the player and system."
- **For Sporefall:** Gives the pair-table vs grammar choice a published vocabulary. A pair table is a "strongly defined recipe" system (full authorial control, every outcome enumerable). A per-mod grammar sits toward "undefined recipes" (rules plus simulation, more experimental play, less control). The census is how Sporefall buys back the control the grammar gives up. "System recognition of outcome" is also a design test: do enemies and arenas respond differently to frost-built and fire-built weapons? If not, one build can dominate.

## How the lane fits together (synthesis, not a source)

- **Why a dominant build fails even when fun:** Juul 2002 ("optimal strategy leads to dull game sessions"), Koster (same tactics means no new challenge), Salen & Zimmerman (degenerate strategies "shortcut" the possibility space), Paul 2011 (choices "distilled to the single best choice"), Mahlmann 2012 and de Mesentier Silva 2019 (a dominant or degenerate strategy is evidence of poor balance, and players lose interest).
- **Why a grammar beats a pair table:** Juul 2002 and Dormans 2012 (a few rules combining; "lean and elegant rule system"), Sweetser & Wiles 2005 (global rules vs hand-scripting), Salen & Zimmerman Ch. 14 (coupled, context-dependent interactions), Grow et al. 2017 (defined vs undefined recipes), Bateni et al. preprint (a 75-card set already needs 5,625 ordered-pair labels).
- **How to detect dominance with the census:** Jaffe 2012 (ban-one-mod win-rate drop), de Mesentier Silva 2019 (per-part "win rate when present"), Fontaine 2019 (quality-diversity: parts that recur across niche elites), Hernandez 2020 (target response graph over builds and arenas), Lantz 2017 (smarter build-choosers should keep winning more), Nelson 2011 (simplest player model that beats the game).
- **Limits of a single fixed bot:** Holmgård 2019 (several persona utility functions), Zook 2014 (active learning to choose what to simulate), Xiao & Yang 2025 (LLM agents track human difficulty in relative, not absolute, terms).
- **Gap:** I found no peer-reviewed work specifically on roguelite build-crafting or Noita-style ordered spell composition. The nearest peer-reviewed evidence is card-game balance (Dominion, Hearthstone) and the crafting design-space paper. Treat the Sporefall census as novel practice, supported by these adjacent literatures.
