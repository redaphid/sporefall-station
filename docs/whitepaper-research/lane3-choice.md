## Iyengar & Lepper (2000): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 79(6), 995-1006. DOI: 10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995
- **URLs opened:** full text PDF https://faculty.washington.edu/jdb/345/345%20Articles/Iyengar%20&%20Lepper%20(2000).pdf (md5 matches the cached `iyengar.pdf`; title and authors on p.1). Metadata (volume, issue, pages, year) confirmed at https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995. Semantic Scholar has no record for this DOI (404).
- **Method/sample (Study 1, field):** tasting booth at Draeger's grocery, Menlo Park, two Saturdays; ~754 shoppers observed; 242 passed the 24-jam display, 260 passed the 6-jam display.
- **Headline numbers (verbatim quotes):**
  - Attraction: "Of the 242 customers who passed the extensive-selection display of jams, 60% (145) actually stopped at the booth. In contrast, of the 260 customers who passed the limited-selection display of jams, only 40% (104) stopped."
  - Sampling did not differ: extensive 1.50 jams vs limited 1.38 jams, "F(1, 245) = 0.83, ns".
  - Purchase: "Nearly 30% (31) of the consumers in the limited-choice condition subsequently purchased a jar of Wilkin & Sons jam; in contrast, only 3% (4) of the consumers in the extensive-choice condition did so" (chi2(1, N = 249) = 32.34, p < .0001).
  - Study 2 (6 vs 30 essay topics): "Of the 70 students assigned to the limited-choice condition, 74% turned in the assignment. In contrast, of the 123 students assigned to the extensive-choice condition, only 60% chose to complete the assignment." Limited-choice essays also scored higher on content.
  - Study 3 (6 vs 30 chocolates, N = 134): chose chocolates as payment: limited 48%, extensive 12%, no-choice 10%.
- **Paraphrase:** A big array draws more attention but converts far worse: 24 jams attracted more browsers, 6 jams sold roughly ten times the rate. Limited-choice participants were also more satisfied.
- **Sporefall:** Supports a small draft (3 mods) over a long list. The effect was in unfamiliar options with no strong prior preference, which is exactly a kid's first runs. Caveat: see the meta-analyses below; the effect is not universal.

## Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37(3), 409-425. DOI: 10.1086/651235
- **URLs opened:** published-version PDF via UNIGE open archive https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:76440 (file link https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/0982778d-0684-4b4a-a3cb-5e89f8387389/download; cover page shows title, authors, 2010, vol. 37 no. 3, pp. 409-425). Metadata confirmed at https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1086/651235 and https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1086/651235. (The cached `scheibehenne.pdf` is the same published text without the cover page; its original download URL was not recorded by the crashed run.)
- **Method/sample:** random-effects meta-analysis plus meta-regression; quote: "In a meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments (N = 5,036), we found a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies."
- **Headline numbers:** mean effect "D = 0.02 (95% confidence interval [CI95] -0.09 to 0.12)"; between-study variance tau2 = 0.12; Q(62) = 192, p < .001.
- **Moderators (Table 2 meta-regression):** consumption as DV b = -.87 (p < .001, more-is-better); expertise or prior preferences b = -.50 (p = .013, reduces overload); journal publication b = +.27 (p = .007, slight publication bias toward overload); publication year b = -.05 (p = .035, later studies find less). Not significant: hypothetical vs real choice, satisfaction as DV, size of the large set (b = .002, p = .140), US vs non-US.
- **Quote:** "decision makers with strong prior preferences or expertise benefit from having more options to choose from" and "no sufficient conditions could be identified that would lead to a reliable occurrence of choice overload."
- **Paraphrase:** Across the literature the average choice-overload effect is about zero, with large unexplained spread. Experts and people with clear preferences do better with more options.
- **Sporefall:** Do not cite the jam study alone as a law. A 3-option draft is justified for novices (kids, early runs) but the evidence allows larger or richer drafts once players know the mods. Prior preference is exactly what boss scouting creates ("I want lightning"), which shrinks overload risk.

## Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U., & Goodman, J. (2015). Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 25(2), 333-358. DOI: 10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002 (online 29 Aug 2014). A corrigendum exists: *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 26(2), 312, DOI: 10.1016/j.jcps.2015.07.001 (Crossref record only; I did not read its content).
- **URLs opened:** full text PDF https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf (md5 matches cached `lane3/chernev.pdf`; running header "Journal of Consumer Psychology 25, 2 (2015) 333-358"). Metadata at https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002 and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jcps.2015.07.001.
- **Method/sample:** meta-analysis, quote: "In a meta-analysis of 99 observations (N = 7202) reported by prior research, we identify four key factors (choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal) that moderate the impact of assortment size on choice overload." (dashes in the original replaced with parentheses)
- **Headline numbers (Table 2):** intercept (no moderators) .41, SE .14, p = .01; choice set complexity .55; decision task difficulty .37; preference uncertainty .32; decision goal .56 (all p < .001). Across choice-from-a-given-assortment studies, larger sets produce more overload: "b = .17, t(39) = 4.5, p < .001"; studies with moderators b = .04 (ns), without moderators b = .41 (p < .001).
- **Quote:** "higher levels of decision task difficulty, greater choice set complexity, higher preference uncertainty, and a more prominent, effort-minimizing goal facilitate choice overload." Also: the significant main effect "is counter to the data reported by prior meta-analytic research, which finds this effect to be nonsignificant (Scheibehenne et al., 2010)."
- **Paraphrase:** Overload is real but conditional. It bites when options are hard to compare, the task is hard (time pressure, many attributes), the chooser does not know what they want, and they want to minimize effort.
- **Sporefall:** All four moderators are high for a kid picking mods mid-run on a phone: time pressure, multi-attribute combos, uncertain preference, effort-minimizing goal. Keep the draft at 3, make each card legible at a glance (one verb per mod), and reduce preference uncertainty by showing the boss weakness before the draft.

## Sid Meier, "Interesting Decisions" (GDC 2012): VERIFIED as a talk record (NOT peer-reviewed; practitioner/folk source)
- **Citation (talk):** Meier, S. (2012). *Interesting Decisions* [Conference session]. Game Developers Conference 2012, Game Design track. Firaxis Games. GDC Vault: https://gdcvault.com/play/1015756/Interesting
- **Citation (coverage):** Alexander, L. (2012, March 7). GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions. *Game Developer* (formerly Gamasutra). https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions
- **URLs opened:** both of the above (fetched raw HTML and read the text). The Vault page confirms title, speaker, affiliation, "GDC 2012", track "Game Design". The article confirms author Leigh Alexander and date March 7, 2012. I did not watch the video (YouTube https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WggIdtrqgKg exists per search, not opened), so all quotes below are the abstract or the reporter's rendering, not a transcript.
- **Origin of the phrase (Meier's own abstract, quote):** "Long, long ago at a GDC far, far away, I introduced the idea that a useful way of looking at gameplay is as "a series of interesting decisions". ... I've never really drilled any deeper into this idea." So the 2012 talk is the elaboration; the original utterance is undated in what I fetched. Do not assign it a year.
- **What makes a decision interesting, per the article (quotes):**
  - Negative test: "If a player always chooses the first from among a set of three choices, it's probably not an interesting choice; nor is a random selection."
  - Tradeoff: "One common characteristic of interesting decisions is that they involve some kind of tradeoff".
  - Situational: "Good decisions are situational. ... when the decision is presented to the player, ideally it acts in an interesting way with the game situation."
  - Play style: "This interesting decision would allow you to express your personal play style."
  - Persistent + informed: "Interesting decisions are persistent and affect the game for a certain amount of time, as long as the player has enough information to make the decision".
  - Categories: risk-versus-reward; short versus long-term; customization.
  - Information: "It's almost worth erring on the side of providing the player with too much information, or at least enough that they're comfortable with understanding the choices."
  - Feedback: "The worst thing you can do is just move on. ... At least have a sound effect that says, 'I've heard what you said and I'm going to do it.'"
  - Pacing: "If you're playing a game with complicated decisions that come at you one after the other the player is going to feel out of control. On the other hand, if you give your player some very simple decisions at a very slow place, they're kind of bored."
  - Setbacks: "You need to prevent setbacks in a very sensitive way, where the player understands why it's happening and what they can do next time".
- **Sporefall:** The 3-mod draft passes Meier's test only if no card is always right and the pick is situational. Boss scouting is the mechanism that makes the draft situational ("lightning now beats fire for this boss"), and his information rule argues for telling kids the weakness plainly rather than hiding it. Give each pick immediate audible/visual confirmation.

## Wardrip-Fruin, Mateas, Dow & Sali (2009): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Wardrip-Fruin, N., Mateas, M., Dow, S., & Sali, S. (2009). Agency reconsidered. In *Proceedings of DiGRA 2009 Conference: Breaking New Ground: Innovation in Games, Play, Practice and Theory*. Digital Games Research Association. DOI: 10.26503/dl.v2009i1.369 (ISSN 2342-9666)
- **URLs opened:** DiGRA Digital Library record https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/369 (meta tags: title, four authors, proceedings title, 2009, DOI). Full text PDF https://eis.ucsc.edu/papers/nwf-C7-digra09-agency.pdf (header "Proceedings of DiGRA 2009", title, authors).
- **Type:** theoretical/design essay, not empirical.
- **Key claim (quote):** agency is "a phenomenon involving both player and game, one that occurs when the actions players desire are among those they can take (and vice versa) as supported by an underlying computational model." Design issues they list (quote): "supporting the actions suggested by the world, helping players transition from their initial expectations to an understanding of how their actions have impact on the model, presenting an interface appropriate to the model, and design that acknowledges the improvisational nature of play."
- **Paraphrase:** Agency is not freedom. It is a match between what the game makes the player want and what the game lets them do.
- **Sporefall:** Scouting a lightning-weak boss creates the desire ("I need lightning"); the draft and mod combos must then offer a way to act on it. If the draft never offers lightning, the evoked desire goes unmet and agency collapses. Argues for a guaranteed or weighted route to the scouted counter.

## Fendt, Harrison, Ware, Cardona-Rivera & Roberts (2012): VERIFIED (empirical agency study)
- **Citation:** Fendt, M. W., Harrison, B., Ware, S. G., Cardona-Rivera, R. E., & Roberts, D. L. (2012). Achieving the illusion of agency. In *Interactive Storytelling: 5th International Conference on Interactive Digital Storytelling (ICIDS 2012)*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science (vol. 7648 per search result; Crossref record shows no volume), pp. 114-125. Springer. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-642-34851-8_11
- **URLs opened:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-642-34851-8_11 (title, five authors, 2012, LNCS, pp. 114-125); https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/978-3-642-34851-8_11 (venue ICIDS, 2012); author preprint PDF https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/fendt2012achieving.pdf (title, authors, abstract, results); BibTeX https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/citation.php?id=fendt2012achieving (notes Best Paper). Springer landing page redirected to a login.
- **Method/sample:** between-subjects, text story with forced-choice points; three versions: branching (Story 1), linear with immediate textual feedback acknowledging choices (Story 2), linear with minimal feedback (Story 3). 79 read the branching story (52 analysed on the matching path), 54 read Story 2, 44 read Story 3; snowball sampling; adults (mean ages 25-28). Wilcoxon rank-sum per survey item.
- **Findings (quote from abstract):** "in a text-based story with forced-choice points there were in most cases no significant difference in players' reported feelings of agency when they experience a branching story vs. a linear story with explicit acknowledgement of their choices." Branching beat minimal-feedback linear on every story-level item (p <= .05). Branching still beat feedback-linear on "I felt that the story would have been different if I had selected different choices" (p = .05).
- **Also (quote, discussion):** higher-agency decisions were those where "the consequences of failure were more severe or that they offered two seemingly distinct story paths", lower-agency ones were "two different means to the same end".
- **Paraphrase:** Much of felt agency comes from the game visibly acknowledging a choice. Choices that are just different routes to the same result feel low-agency.
- **Sporefall:** Each drafted mod needs immediate, legible acknowledgement (visual change to the weapon, a callout). The boss weakness payoff (big lightning hit numbers or stagger) is the "consequence" that makes the pick feel real. Mods that feel like "different means to the same end" (e.g., two similar damage boosts) will read as low-agency. Caveat: adult sample, text stories, not action games or children.

## Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008): VERIFIED (added; choice meta-analysis with children and option counts)
- **Citation:** Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. (2008). The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes: A meta-analysis of research findings. *Psychological Bulletin*, 134(2), 270-300. DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270. PMID 18298272.
- **URLs opened:** PubMed abstract via https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=18298272&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (journal, date, volume, pages, authors, abstract); Crossref search confirmed the DOI; full text PDF https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf (Table 5 read).
- **Method/sample (quote):** "A meta-analysis of 41 studies examined the effect of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes in a variety of settings with both child and adult samples."
- **Findings (abstract quote):** "providing choice enhanced intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance, and perceived competence". Effect stronger "(b) when 2 to 4 successive choices were given, (c) when rewards were not given after the choice manipulation ... (e) for children compared to adults".
- **Numbers from Table 5 and text (fixed effects, random effects in parentheses):** options per choice: two d = 0.20 (0.19), three to five d = 0.38 (0.43), more than five d = 0.26 (0.34); between-group test only marginal (Qb = 5.62, marked with a dagger; RE 3.29, ns). Total choices: 2-4 choices d = 0.61 vs one choice d = 0.21 vs five or more d = 0.32. Instructionally irrelevant choices d = 0.59 (0.61), the largest choice-type effect. Children (preschool to 12th grade) d = 0.55 vs adults d = 0.25 under fixed effects, but no significant difference under random effects (p < .10).
- **Paraphrase:** Choice reliably helps motivation. The sweet spot in this literature was a few successive choices with 3-5 options each; more than 5 options did worse than 3-5 (not significantly). The child advantage is fixed-effects only.
- **Sporefall:** A floor-by-floor draft of 3 options is a "successive choices, 3-5 options" design, the best-performing cell here. Do not attach an extra reward to the act of choosing; let the mod itself be the payoff.

## Fiorillo, Tobler & Schultz (2003): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Fiorillo, C. D., Tobler, P. N., & Schultz, W. (2003). Discrete coding of reward probability and uncertainty by dopamine neurons. *Science*, 299(5614), 1898-1902. DOI: 10.1126/science.1077349. PMID 12649484.
- **URLs opened:** PubMed abstract via https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=12649484,31209310,29952052&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (journal, date 2003 Mar 21, vol/issue/pages, authors, abstract); Semantic Scholar https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1126/science.1077349 (cached `ss_10.1126_science.1077349.json`).
- **Method:** not detailed in the abstract I read, which says only "Using distinct stimuli to indicate the probability of reward"; species and recording method not confirmed from a page I opened.
- **Findings (quote):** uncertainty "is determined by probability P, being maximal at P = 0.5 and decreasing at higher and lower probabilities." "the phasic activation of dopamine neurons varied monotonically across the full range of probabilities, supporting past claims that this response codes the discrepancy between predicted and actual reward. In contrast, a previously unobserved response covaried with uncertainty and consisted of a gradual increase in activity until the potential time of reward."
- **Paraphrase:** Two signals: one tracks how surprising the reward is (prediction error), a second ramps up during anticipation and peaks when the outcome is a coin flip.
- **Sporefall:** Mechanistic reason uncertain rewards feel compelling. The same property is what loot boxes exploit. For a kids' game with no money in the loop, the design rule is: use uncertainty inside play (which mods will the draft show), not as a pay or grind hook.

- **Citation:** Drummond, A., & Sauer, J. D. (2018). Video game loot boxes are psychologically akin to gambling. *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2(8), 530-532. DOI: 10.1038/s41562-018-0360-1. Article type on the page: "Comment" (dc.type "BriefCommunication").
- **URLs opened:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0360-1 (via curl with cookies; meta tags show title, volume 2, issue 8, pp. 530-532, online 2018-06-18; body paywalled); PubMed record (above eutils URL, confirms authors, journal, pages); figshare record https://api.figshare.com/v2/articles/22969859 (no file attached).
- **Standfirst (quote from the Nature page):** "Video games are increasingly exposing young players to randomized in-game reward mechanisms, purchasable for real money" ... "so-called loot boxes. Do loot boxes constitute a form of gambling?"
- **Findings (secondary: Massey University impact story, https://www.massey.ac.nz/research/research-impact-stories/protecting-vulnerable-gamers/, raw HTML read):** "Dr Drummond and Dr Sauer looked at 22 video games with loot boxes released on the major consoles in 2016 and 2017. - **Sporefall:** Sporefall's random drops fail criterion 1 (no money or tradable value is staked) and criterion 4 (nothing is lost by not participating), so they are not loot boxes under this framework. Keep it that way: no currency that buys random rolls.

## King & Delfabbro (2018): METADATA + ABSTRACT VERIFIED (editorial)
- **Citation:** King, D. L., & Delfabbro, P. H. (2018). Predatory monetization schemes in video games (e.g. 'loot boxes') and internet gaming disorder. *Addiction*, 113(11), 1967-1969. DOI: 10.1111/add.14286. PMID 29952052. (Editorial, per the university press release.)
- **URLs opened:** PubMed record (eutils URL above: Addiction 2018 Nov;113(11):1967-1969, authors, Epub 2018 Jun 28); https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1111/add.14286 (abstract); EurekAlert press release from University of Adelaide, 28 Jun 2018, https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/462377 (raw HTML read). - **Definition (abstract quote, OpenAlex):** "Predatory monetization schemes in video games are purchasing systems that disguise or withhold the longterm cost of the activity until players are already financially and psychologically committed."
- **Tactics (press release quote):** "limited disclosure of the product, unavoidable solicitations, and manipulation of reward outcomes to encourage purchasing behaviours over skilful play"; "psychological 'entrapment' where players spend an escalating amount of money because they believe they have invested too much to quit"; "'limited time' offers". ## Zendle & Cairns (2018): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Zendle, D., & Cairns, P. (2018). Video game loot boxes are linked to problem gambling: Results of a large-scale survey. *PLOS ONE*, 13(11), e0206767. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0206767. Correction published: *PLOS ONE* 14(3), e0214167 (2019).
- **URLs opened:** https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0206767 (raw HTML; meta tags: title, authors, PLOS ONE, vol 13, issue 11, e0206767, Nov 21, 2018; correction notice on the page).
- **Method/sample:** online survey, self-selected gamers aged 18+, recruited via reddit, unpaid; problem gambling measured with the 9-item Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI).
- **Findings (abstract quote):** "A large-scale survey of gamers (n = 7,422) found evidence for a link (η2 = 0.054) between the amount that gamers spent on loot boxes and the severity of their problem gambling. - **Sporefall:** The risk attaches to paid random boxes specifically, not to paid items in general and not to randomness per se. Supports: random drops fine, never sell them.

## Zendle, Meyer & Over (2019): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Zendle, D., Meyer, R., & Over, H. (2019). Adolescents and loot boxes: Links with problem gambling and motivations for purchase. *Royal Society Open Science*, 6(6), 190049. DOI: 10.1098/rsos.190049. PMCID PMC6599795.

## Loewenstein (1994): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. *Psychological Bulletin*, 116(1), 75-98. DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75
- **URLs opened:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75 (title, author, July 1994, vol 116(1), 75-98); full text (OCR'd scan) https://www.byrdseed.com/handouts/Psychology%20of%20Curiosity.pdf (header "Psychological Bulletin 1994 Vol. 116, No. 1, 75-98"). The author-hosted scan https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf opened but is image-only.
- **Type:** theoretical review.
- **Core claim (abstract quote):** "The new account interprets curiosity as a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding." Body: "an information gap can be defined by two quantities: what one knows and what one wants to know."
- **Counterintuitive prediction (quote):** "curiosity should be positively related to one's knowledge in a particular domain", illustrated by someone who knows 47 of 50 state capitals framing it as "not knowing 3 state capitals".
- **Situational triggers he lists (quotes):** "1. The posing of a question or presentation of a riddle or puzzle"; "2. Exposure to a sequence of events with an anticipated but unknown resolution", which is heightened "when an individual generates a prediction or forecast of the outcome"; "3 The violation of expectations"; "4 Possession of information by someone else also causes curiosity."
- **Also:** guessing combined with accuracy feedback raised curiosity more than either alone (reported study of guessing US-state extremes).
- **Sporefall:** Boss scouting is a textbook information-gap trigger: show a boss silhouette and a "weak to ?" slot before the floor. Partial knowledge ("it flinched at a spark") beats none. Letting kids guess the weakness and then confirming it combines prediction with feedback. In co-op, one player scouting and knowing (trigger 4) pulls the others in.

## To, Ali, Kaufman & Hammer (2016): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** To, A., Ali, S., Kaufman, G., & Hammer, J. (2016). Integrating curiosity and uncertainty in game design. In *Proceedings of the First International Joint Conference of DiGRA and FDG (DiGRA/FDG 2016)*. Digital Games Research Association. DOI: 10.26503/dl.v2016i1.793
- **URLs opened:** DiGRA Digital Library record https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/793 (meta tags: title, four authors, "Proceedings of DiGRA/FDG 2016 Conference", 2016, DOI); full text PDF https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/793/793/790 (title, authors, Carnegie Mellon University). The PDF text layer drops spaces; quotes below have spaces restored, words unchanged.
- **Type:** theoretical/design analysis, not empirical.
- **Findings (abstract quote):** "Curiosity as a psychological state or trait is characterized by a preference for uncertainty that motivates responses such as exploring, manipulating, and questioning. ... game designers can thus induce curiosity by creating or increasing the salience of information gaps." It reviews "five key types of curiosity: perceptual curiosity, manipulatory curiosity, curiosity about the complex or ambiguous, conceptual curiosity, and adjustive-reactive curiosity" and ties them to "Costikyan's well-known theory of uncertainty in games, proposing that designers can employ uncertainty to motivate, manipulate, and accommodate players' curiosity levels."
- **Conclusion (quote):** "players have differing tolerance for uncertainty and that situational factors can affect that tolerance. For example, players' confidence in their ability to close a knowledge gap makes them more tolerant of uncertainty."
- **Children:** they cite a validated game-based curiosity measure for children (Jirout & Klahr 2012, "Underwater Exploration!"), in which children choose between windows with no, medium, or maximum uncertainty; and suggest games offer "successive choices with differing levels of uncertainty early in the game" to gauge a player's curiosity. The model they build on ties inability to tolerate gaps to "helplessness, frustration, or anger."
- **Sporefall:** Uncertainty helps only when the player believes they can close the gap. For young kids, scouting must reliably yield an answer (never a dead end), and the draft should always include at least one option they understand. Two nephews may differ in uncertainty tolerance; co-op lets the curious one scout while the other builds.

## Gómez-Maureira, Kniestedt, van Duijn, Rieffe & Plaat (2021): VERIFIED (added; empirical game-curiosity study)

## Lane synthesis for Sporefall (my inference from the verified sources above)
- **Draft size 3 is well supported for kids, with a caveat.** Iyengar & Lepper show overload in unfamiliar domains; Chernev et al. show the four aggravating conditions (complexity, difficulty, preference uncertainty, effort-minimizing goal) are all present mid-run on a phone; Patall et al. found 3-5 options and a few successive choices performed best. Scheibehenne et al. warn the average overload effect is about zero and experts benefit from more options, so a larger draft could be an unlock for experienced players, not the default.
- **Scouting is the lever that makes the draft interesting.** It turns a context-free pick into a situational one (Meier), creates the desire that agency satisfies (Wardrip-Fruin et al.), reduces preference uncertainty (Chernev et al.), and is an information-gap trigger (Loewenstein). Curiosity peaks near 50/50 confidence (Kang et al.), so hint at two candidate weaknesses rather than revealing one outright or showing nothing.
- **Guarantee the scouted counter is reachable.** Evoked desire with no available action breaks agency (Wardrip-Fruin et al.), and uncertainty is tolerated only when players believe they can close the gap (To et al.).
- **Acknowledge every pick loudly.** Visible feedback carries much of felt agency (Fendt et al.; Meier's "at least have a sound effect").
- **Random drops are fine; selling them is not.** Harm links attach to paid random boxes, more strongly in adolescents (Zendle & Cairns; Zendle, Meyer & Over). Avoid the predatory tactics King & Delfabbro list even without money: manipulated odds, timed pressure, sunk-cost hooks, rewards that replace skilful play.
