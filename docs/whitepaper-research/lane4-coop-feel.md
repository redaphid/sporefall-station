## 1. Failure and learning

### Juul, J. (2013). The Art of Failure: An Essay on the Pain of Playing Video Games. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (Playful Thinking series). 157 pp. ISBN 978-0-262-01905-7.
- Metadata confirmed at: https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Art_of_Failure.html?id=5sC1knAj_isC (title, Jesper Juul, MIT Press, 2013, ISBN 0262019051 / 9780262019057, 157 pp., Playful Thinking). Also https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2013/02/25/the-art-of-failure/ (author's post, Feb 2013, "fresh out on MIT Press"). Book text excerpt (ch. 1) opened at https://www.salon.com/2013/07/13/video_games_make_us_all_losers/ ("Copyright 2013 MIT Press").
- MIT Press page (mitpress.mit.edu/9780262529952) returned HTTP 403, not used.
- Quote (Salon excerpt of ch. 1): "This is the paradox of failure in games. It can be stated like this: 1. We generally avoid failure. 2. We experience failure when playing games. 3. We seek out games, although we will experience something that we normally avoid."
- Quote: "failure is an integral element of the overall experience of playing a game, a motivator, something that helps us reconsider our strategies and see the strategic depth in a game, a clear proof that we have improved when we finally overcome it."
- Quote: "while games uniquely induce such feelings of being inadequate, they also motivate us to play more in order to escape the same inadequacy, and the feeling of escaping failure (often by improving our skills) is central to the enjoyment of games. Games promise us a fair chance of redeeming ourselves."
- Quote: "good games tend to offer well-defined goals and clear feedback. This gives us an objective measure of our performance, and allows us to optimize our strategies."
- Paraphrase: failure is tolerable and motivating when it reads as the player's own, fixable shortfall and the game offers a fair chance to redeem it. Failure is useful because it prompts a strategy change. Essay/argument, not empirical.
- Sporefall: a boss death is only productive if the kids can see *why* they lost (which attack, which mod was wrong), so the next run's mod choice ("bring lightning") is a strategy change, not a reroll. Telegraphs plus a clear death cause are what make boss failure "fair".

### Hunicke, R. (2005). The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games. In Proceedings of the 2005 ACM SIGCHI International Conference on Advances in Computer Entertainment Technology (ACE '05), Valencia, Spain, pp. 429-433. ACM. doi:10.1145/1178477.1178573
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/1178477.1178573 (title, Robin Hunicke, Northwestern University, ACE05, pp. 429-433, 2005-06-15). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1145/1178477.1178573.
- Full text not opened: the ACM PDF and landing page returned 403 / a bot-verification page when tried; no open-access copy found (Unpaywall/OpenAlex: closed). Findings below are limited to the abstract.
- Quote (abstract): "Conventional wisdom suggests that while players enjoy unpredictability or novelty during gameplay experiences, they will feel \"cheated\" if games are adjusted during or across play sessions. In order for adjustment to be effective, it must be performed without disrupting or degrading the core player experience."
- Quote (abstract): the paper "presents an interactive DDA system (Hamlet), and offers preliminary evaluation results which challenge common assumptions about player enjoyment and adjustment dynamics."
- Paraphrase: in-session difficulty adjustment has to work without disrupting the core experience; the preliminary Hamlet results "challenge common assumptions about player enjoyment and adjustment dynamics". Which assumptions, the numbers, and the sample were not verified (full text not opened).
- Sporefall: mixed-skill co-op (adult plus 8-12 year olds) is a DDA case. Adjust quietly (boss tempo, telegraph length, resource drops) rather than via visible "easy mode" that could make a kid feel singled out. Do not cite specific Hamlet results without the full text.

### Empirical: failure and enjoyment

#### Petralito, S., Brühlmann, F., Iten, G., Mekler, E. D., & Opwis, K. (2017). A Good Reason to Die: How Avatar Death and High Challenges Enable Positive Experiences. In Proceedings of the 2017 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '17), pp. 5087-5097. ACM. doi:10.1145/3025453.3026047
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3025453.3026047 (title + subtitle, five authors, CHI '17, pp. 5087-5097, 2017). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3026047. Full text not opened (closed access).
- Method/sample (abstract): survey of 95 participants right after the release of Dark Souls III; open questions plus player-experience measures.
- Quote (abstract): "Players generally enjoyed challenging play sessions and mostly reported positive experiences, with achievement and learning moments strongly contributing to positive experiences. However, these factors themselves were enabled by negative events such as difficulties and avatar death."
- Paraphrase: in a very hard, pattern-learning action game, death and difficulty were the precondition for the moments players valued most (achievement, learning). Adult, self-selected fans of a notoriously hard game; applying this to 8-12 year olds is an extrapolation.
- Sporefall: boss deaths can be good if each one teaches something (a learnable pattern). This needs readable, consistent boss attacks; unreadable deaths remove the "learning moment" the positive experience depends on.

#### Frommel, J., Klarkowski, M., & Mandryk, R. L. (2021). The Struggle is Spiel: On Failure and Success in Games. In FDG '21: The 16th International Conference on the Foundations of Digital Games, pp. 1-12. ACM. doi:10.1145/3472538.3472565
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3472538.3472565 (title, three authors, FDG 2021, pp. 1-12, 2021-08-03). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1145/3472538.3472565 (an OA copy is listed at https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/420615, not opened).
- Method/sample (abstract): mixed-methods study; 182 participants described scenarios of success and failure.
- Quote (abstract): "failure should be differentiated into temporary and perpetual failure, and that temporary failure (the struggle) is integral to the experience of success (overcoming the struggle)."
- Quote (abstract): "although success is generally more enjoyable than failure, players who enjoy a challenge are more likely to experience competence and find enjoyment within experiences of failure."
- Paraphrase: temporary failure that ends in success is part of the fun; perpetual failure is not. Tolerance for failure varies by player, which matters in a mixed-age group.
- Sporefall: design the boss loop so a losing run converts to a winning one within a session (temporary failure). A kid who fails repeatedly with no visible progress is in "perpetual failure"; telegraphs and prep hints ("weak to lightning") shorten the path from struggle to success.

#### Iacovides, I., Cox, A. L., McAndrew, P., Aczel, J. C., & Scanlon, E. (2015). Game-Play Breakdowns and Breakthroughs: Exploring the Relationship Between Action, Understanding, and Involvement. Human-Computer Interaction, 30(3-4), 202-231. doi:10.1080/07370024.2014.987347 (published online 22 Dec 2014)
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/07370024.2014.987347 (title, five authors, Human-Computer Interaction, vol. 30, issue 3-4, pp. 202-231). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1080/07370024.2014.987347. Accepted manuscript listed at https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1455605/1/cox_HCI_breaks-accepted.pdf (not opened).
- Method/sample (abstract): multiple case study; mix of gamers and casual players; interviews, observations of play, post-play cued interviews, diaries. Output: 14 refined claims.
- Quote (abstract): "The claims emphasize how players experience learning via breakthroughs in understanding, where involvement is increased when the player feels responsible for progress."
- Paraphrase: breakdowns (in action or understanding) are where learning starts; involvement rises when the player's own understanding produces the breakthrough.
- Sporefall: the "prepare for a lightning-weak boss" loop is a designed breakdown-then-breakthrough. The kid who realises "lightning plus wet" and sees it work should feel it was their insight, which argues for visible cause and effect rather than hidden multipliers.

### Telegraphing and readable attacks (no peer-reviewed study found; practitioner sources, clearly labelled)

#### [Practitioner, not peer-reviewed] Stout, M. (2015, September 2). Enemy Attacks and Telegraphing. Game Developer (formerly Gamasutra), Featured Blogs.
- Opened and confirmed at: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/enemy-attacks-and-telegraphing (title, author Mike Stout, datePublished 2015-09-02 in page metadata).
- Quote: "If players don't understand the questions they are being asked, they actually can not play your game."
- Quote: "Before the attack, we need a small delay . The purpose of this delay is to tell the player \"Okay, here I come. I'm gonna attack now... Can you avoid my damage? \" Then, if the sword comes down and the player takes damage, it's not because they didn't know what they were being asked to do. It becomes fair."
- Paraphrase: in avoidance-based combat each attack is a question; the telegraph (wind-up animation, or for projectiles a charge-up mix of animation, sound, VO and VFX) is what makes being hit fair. No timing numbers given.
- Sporefall: every boss attack needs a pre-attack delay plus a multi-channel tell (animation, sound, colour). Voice or sound cues matter for kids looking at their own phone, not the shared scene.

#### [Practitioner, GDC talk, not peer-reviewed] Keren, I. (2018). Boss Up: Boss Battle Design Fundamentals and Retrospective. Game Developers Conference (GDC) 2018, Design track. Untame.
- Opened: GDC Vault page https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024921/Boss-Up-Boss-Battle-Design (title, Itay Keren, Untame, GDC 2018, Design track) and slide deck https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/Keren_Itay_BossUp.pdf (116 pages, text extracted).
- Quote (slides, summary page): "Keep attacks predictable, telegraphed (or at least memorizable). Make sure player goals, progression and hits are clearly presented. Players tend to give up when they are helpless, or perceive themselves to be."
- Quote (slides): "Don't plan how to kill the players; plan how to keep them alive and design attacks around that negative space."
- Other slide points (verbatim fragments): "Glowing weak spot = clear vulnerability"; "If unavoidable, telegraph"; "Previous wave telegraphs the next one"; "Anticipated failure → Anticipated triumph"; multi-phase: "Teach, then test".
- Paraphrase: a good boss is predictable and telegraphed (shadows, wind-up animation, vocal warnings, slow motion), exposes a clear weak spot, is built around safe space rather than kill zones, and eases toward a visible end so players anticipate winning.
- Sporefall: a lightning-weak boss should *show* its weakness in the fight (a glowing element-coloured weak spot, a stagger when hit by lightning) and preview it before the fight so the mod draft has a target. Telegraphs plus a visible "phase nearly done" signal fit the owner's goal directly.

## 2. Co-op and co-located play

### Gajadhar, B. J., de Kort, Y. A. W., & IJsselsteijn, W. A. (2008). Shared Fun Is Doubled Fun: Player Enjoyment as a Function of Social Setting. In P. Markopoulos, B. de Ruyter, W. IJsselsteijn, & D. Rowland (Eds.), Fun and Games 2008 (Second International Conference, Eindhoven, 20-21 Oct 2008), Lecture Notes in Computer Science 5294, pp. 106-117. Berlin: Springer. doi:10.1007/978-3-540-88322-7_11
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/shared-fun-is-doubled-fun-player-enjoyment-as-a-function-of-socia/ (TU/e research portal: title, authors, editors, LNCS vol. 5294, pp. 106-117, 2008, DOI). Cross-checked: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-540-88322-7_11. Springer landing page blocked (bot challenge); full text not opened.
- Method (abstract): self-reports on the Game Experience Questionnaire (GEQ) and a state aggression questionnaire across three configurations: virtual, mediated, and co-located co-play; also familiarity and winning vs. losing. Sample size not shown in the abstract.
- Quote (abstract): "Results indicate that a co-located co-player significantly adds to the fun, challenge, and perceived competence in the game. Aggression was influenced by familiarity and performance. The effect of social context was mediated by the level of social presence."
- Related, same authors: Gajadhar, de Kort & IJsselsteijn (2008), "Influence of social setting on player experience of digital games", CHI '08 Extended Abstracts, pp. 3099-3104, doi:10.1145/1358628.1358814 (Crossref + OpenAlex abstract: "significant differences in playing against a computer, a mediated other, or a co-located other on player experience in terms of positive affect, competence, tension and hostility").
- Paraphrase: playing with someone physically present raised enjoyment, challenge and felt competence compared with a computer or remote co-player; the effect ran through social presence.
- Sporefall: same-room play is Sporefall's biggest asset. Design for the room (shout-able callouts, shared moments on every screen, reactions visible to everyone) rather than for each phone in isolation.

### Rocha, J. B., Mascarenhas, S., & Prada, R. (2008). Game Mechanics for Cooperative Games. In N. Zagalo & R. Prada (Eds.), Actas da Conferência ZON | Digital Games 2008, pp. 72-80 (page numbers as printed in the PDF). Centro de Estudos de Comunicação e Sociedade, Universidade do Minho. ISBN 978-989-95500-2-5.
- Opened: OJS landing page http://www.lasics.uminho.pt/ojs/index.php/zondgames08/article/view/343 (title, three authors, 2008 proceedings, abstract) and full-text PDF http://www.lasics.uminho.pt/ojs/index.php/zondgames08/article/download/343/319 (editors, ISBN, page numbers). No DOI.
- Patterns named (verbatim headings): "Complementarity", "Synergies between abilities", "Abilities that can only be used on another player", "Shared Goals", "Synergies between goals (Interlaced/Intertwined goals)", "Special Rules for Players of the same Team".
- Quote: "some of the abilities of one character type have some synergy with abilities of another character type. Some examples of this exist in World of Warcraft - a shadow priest (who deals mostly shadow damage) can cause an enemy to become more vulnerable to shadow damage, which also causes an increase of damage that the warlocks are causing".
- Method/sample: preliminary questionnaire at a university games showing; 16 participants (all male; 6 occasional, 10 frequent players); two-player game Geometry Friends. Ratings on a 1-6 scale: Fun 4.5, Need for Cooperation 5.6. Quote: "all the players felt the need to cooperate in order to accomplish the goal of each one of the levels." Designers also worried a support character felt "not interesting enough by itself" and redesigned it to be active.
- Sporefall: "synergies between abilities" is exactly wet (frost/water) plus lightning across two players. Make cross-player combos a named, visible pattern: one kid applies the status, the other cashes it in. Avoid a pure support role; the paper's own playtest flagged it as boring.

### Seif El-Nasr, M., Aghabeigi, B., Milam, D., Erfani, M., Lameman, B., Maygoli, H., & Mah, S. (2010). Understanding and evaluating cooperative games. In Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '10), pp. 253-262. ACM. doi:10.1145/1753326.1753363
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/1753326.1753363 (title, seven authors, CHI '10, pp. 253-262, 2010-04-10). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753363. Full text not opened (OA status closed; no author copy found).
- Method/sample (abstract): patterns derived from analysis of 14 cooperative games; study with 60 participants in groups of 2-3 playing Rock Band 2, Lego Star Wars, Kameo and Little Big Planet; session video annotated with Cooperative Performance Metrics (CPMs), validated by inter-rater agreement.
- Quote (abstract): "Videos of the play sessions were annotated using the CPMs, which were then mapped to cooperative patterns that caused them. Results, validated through inter-rater agreement, identify several effective cooperative patterns and lessons for future cooperative game designs."
- Pattern names and CPM results are not quoted from the paper itself (full text not opened); the companion thesis below gives them.
- Sporefall: supports treating observable co-op behaviours as a playtest metric. For live playtests, code boss-fight recordings with the CPMs listed under the thesis below.

#### Companion source for Seif El-Nasr et al. (2010): Aghabeigi, B. (2011). Understanding and Evaluating Cooperative Video Games. MSc thesis, School of Interactive Arts and Technology, Simon Fraser University (senior supervisor: M. Seif El-Nasr; defended 21 Feb 2011).
- Opened and read: https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/11576/etd6462_BAghabeigi.pdf (134 pp.; title page, approval page). This is the thesis version of the same study as the CHI 2010 paper (same 4 games, same 60 players, same CPMs). Cite it for details; it is not peer-reviewed.
- Sample (thesis text): "a study involving 60 kids ages 8-12 who were asked to play 4 different cooperative games"; "over 60 kids ages 8-12 in groups of 2-3 per session, resulting in 23 sessions". Note: the results chapter elsewhere says "the age group we had (6-14)", so the reported age range is inconsistent within the thesis.
- CPMs (section headings): Laughter and Excitement Together; Worked Out Strategies; Helping; Global Strategies; Waited for Each Other; Got in Each Others' Way.
- Patterns added beyond Rocha et al. (verbatim names): Camera Setting; Interacting with the same object; Shared Puzzles; Shared Characters; Special characters targeting lone wolf; Vocalization; Limited resources.
- Quote: "complementarity, shared goals, shared puzzles, and shared objects had a major impact on the identified CPMs."
- Quote: "Helping occurred when the game was difficult for players" ... "the number of events observed was significantly higher for Kameo, which was rated the most difficult game by our participants. Thus, this CPM is directly tied to difficulty and can be used to tune difficulty of the game."
- Quote: "for the age group we had (6-14), split screen and camera led by the first player caused Waited for Each Other and Got in Each Others' Way CPMs, which may have a negative impact on the play experience."
- Quote: "visual style and animation as well as cut scenes caused much of the Laughter and Excitement Together".
- Vocalization pattern (Left 4 Dead): characters automatically call out danger, which "encourages players to play close together and support each other."
- Sporefall: this is the closest match in the lane (kids 8-12, co-located co-op). Predictions: (1) a hard boss produces helping, so difficulty can be tuned by watching for it; (2) complementary roles and shared targets (one boss, one weak spot) drive joint strategy; (3) automatic callouts ("Boss is charging lightning!") fit a no-voice-chat, same-room game; (4) spectacle and animation drive shared laughter, which supports making the wet-plus-lightning reaction big and visible on every phone; (5) since each player has their own phone, avoid forcing one player to wait on another.

### Harris, J., Hancock, M., & Scott, S. D. (2016). Leveraging Asymmetries in Multiplayer Games: Investigating Design Elements of Interdependent Play. In Proceedings of the 2016 Annual Symposium on Computer-Human Interaction in Play (CHI PLAY '16), pp. 350-361. ACM. doi:10.1145/2967934.2968113
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/2967934.2968113 (title + subtitle, three authors, CHI PLAY '16, pp. 350-361, 2016-10-15). Full text opened: https://markhancock.ca/pmwiki/uploads/Publications/p350-harris.pdf (author-hosted, 12 pp.).
- Method/sample: in-lab study of the asymmetric game Beam Me 'Round Scotty! (Kirk on gamepad, Scotty on mouse/keyboard); "Thirty-four participants (8 female) were recruited in pairs" from a university (all aged 18-29), pairs with a prior relationship; about one hour each; thematic analysis of recordings and interviews. Adults, not children.
- Framework (verbatim terms): asymmetries of Ability, Challenge, Interface, Information, Investment, Goal/Responsibility; dependence Mirrored / Unidirectional / Bidirectional ("AKA Symbiosis"); timing Asynchronous / Sequential (Disjoint) / Expectant / Concurrent / Coincident.
- Quote: "there is a general increase in \"interesting-ness\" (or at least the difficulty of execution) as one progresses down these lists. For example, actions with coincident timing are distinctly harder to execute than those with disjoint sequential timing."
- Quote (their example of sequential timing): "one player removes the protective casing from an armoured enemy with a grenade, allowing the second player to finish the enemy off at their leisure."
- Quote (abstract): asymmetric play targets pairs "such as grandparents and grandchildren, highly skilled players and novices".
- Quote (discussion): "If the normal social dynamic of the pair were deliberately reversed (e.g. a child in the leadership role with their parents as subordinates), such an asymmetric game could be employed as a role-taking exercise." Also: "tighter coupling or more exacting synchronization between players' action will be more engaging for highly skilled players, we have not specifically tested or quantified such experiences."
- Paraphrase: asymmetric roles let players of different skill play together; interdependence can be tuned by direction and timing. Sequential timing is the easiest to execute; coincident timing is the hardest and is suggested for skilled pairs.
- Sporefall: wet-then-lightning is "sequential (disjoint) timing" with bidirectional potential. It is the forgiving form a kid can pull off; avoid "coincident" combos that need frame-tight simultaneity from an 8-year-old. Asymmetry of information (e.g., the kid who sees the boss's weakness preview calls the plan) could put a kid in the lead role.

### Beznosyk, A., Quax, P., Lamotte, W., & Coninx, K. (2012). The Effect of Closely-Coupled Interaction on Player Experience in Casual Games. In M. Herrlich, R. Malaka, & M. Masuch (Eds.), Entertainment Computing - ICEC 2012, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 7522, pp. 243-255. Berlin, Heidelberg: Springer. doi:10.1007/978-3-642-33542-6_21
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-33542-6_21 (opened in a browser: authors, LNCS vol. 7522, editors, 2012, DOI). Pages from https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-642-33542-6_21 (243-255). Full text opened: https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/iwec/icec2012/BeznosykQLC12.pdf (IFIP open library).
- Method/sample: "thirty-six unpaid volunteers (thirty-one males and five females)", mean age 28 (21-38), mostly computer-science staff and students; pairs in neighbouring rooms on networked laptops with no communication possible; six custom games, one cooperative pattern each; ratings on visual analogue scales for excitement, engagement, challenge, understandability, replayability.
- Definition (verbatim): "If a game requires a lot of waiting or if the actions of one player directly affect the other player, it was categorized as the first type [closely-coupled]. The games that do not require tight collaboration between players and allow more independent performance were assigned to the second type [loosely-coupled]."
- Quote (abstract): "most patterns with closely-coupled interaction have shown an overall higher user evaluation than loosely-coupled, satisfying criteria of casual games."
- Quote (conclusion): "games 2 (complementary roles pattern) and 3 (interaction with the same object pattern) satisfied all 5 criteria considered in the study". The limited-resources closely-coupled game did not do as well: "not all closely-coupled games were evaluated equally."

"showed a higher contribution of several loosely-coupled patterns to the overall player experience (e.g. shared puzzles), while in our case close coupling was more preferred among players. - Sporefall: Sporefall players are co-located and can talk, so the authors' own caveat applies. Tight coupling through a shared target (the boss's weak spot) plus complementary roles is the promising form; limited-resource coupling (making players wait on each other) was the weaker variant. Adults only; not tested with children.

## 3. Parent-child and family co-play

### Takeuchi, L., & Stevens, R. (2011). The New Coviewing: Designing for Learning through Joint Media Engagement. New York: The Joan Ganz Cooney Center at Sesame Workshop and LIFE Center. (Fall 2011; with B. Barron, E. Branch-Ridley, M. Brooks, H. Cooperman, A. Fenwick-Naditch, S. Fisch, R. Herr-Stephenson, C. Llorente, S. Mehus, S. Pasnik, W. Penuel, G. Revelle.) Report, not peer-reviewed.
- Opened and read: https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/jgc_coviewing_desktop.pdf (75 pp.; cover shows title, "Fall 2011", "The Joan Ganz Cooney Center at Sesame Workshop and LIFE Center", "By Lori Takeuchi and Reed Stevens").
- Definition quoted in the report (Stevens & Penuel, 2010): "Joint media engagement (JME) refers to spontaneous and designed experiences of people using media together."
- Conditions for productive JME (verbatim headings): Mutual engagement; Dialogic inquiry; Co-creation; Boundary crossing; Intention to develop; Focus on content, not control.
- Quote (Mutual engagement): "Younger and older, more and less knowledgeable, distant and near partners are equally motivated to participate in the activity and find it sufficiently appealing and/or challenging to sustain engagement throughout. Neither partner is bored nor participates out of sheer obligation to the other."
- Quote (Differentiation of roles, design principle 3): "Assigning roles to participants so that tasks and content match up to individual maturity is another way of ensuring that everyone is suitably challenged and/or entertained. ... Have partners work toward a common goal together, and force them to talk to coordinate their efforts. Interaction often needs to be engineered this way; less structure may fail to elicit dialogic inquiry."
- Quote (Focus on content, not control): "Language such as \"Don't touch!\" or \"Wait, not yet.\" are kept to a minimum, as are scolding gestures and jockeying for controllers."
- Listed challenge: "For the most part, adults and children don't enjoy the same content." Design principle 2 "Multiple planes of engagement" answers it (Sesame Street layering adult and child humour).
- Method: six case studies plus author discussions; principles focus on young children and adults.
- Sporefall: the uncle is not the target player, so the game must hold him too (multiple planes). Give each player a role matched to maturity (e.g., the adult manages a harder mod or reads boss tells aloud; kids own the big visible payoff), and engineer a shared goal that needs talk ("you wet it, I zap it"). Each player on their own phone removes controller-jockeying.

### Siyahhan, S., Barab, S. A., & Downton, M. P. (2010). Using activity theory to understand intergenerational play: The case of Family Quest. International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning, 5(4), 415-432. doi:10.1007/s11412-010-9097-1

### Coyne, S. M., Padilla-Walker, L. M., Stockdale, L., & Day, R. D. (2011). Game On... Girls: Associations Between Co-playing Video Games and Adolescent Behavioral and Family Outcomes. Journal of Adolescent Health, 49(2), 160-165. doi:10.1016/j.jadohealth.2010.11.249. PMID 21783048.
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=21783048&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PubMed record). Cross-check: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2010.11.249.
- Method/sample (abstract): 287 adolescents and their parents; questionnaires, mostly child, mother and father reports; correlational.
- Quote (abstract): "co-playing video games with parents was associated with decreased levels of internalizing and aggressive behaviors, and heightened prosocial behavior for girls only. Co-playing video games was also marginally related to parent-child connectedness for girls, even after controlling for age-inappropriate games played with parents."
- Paraphrase: correlational. Parent co-play was linked to better outcomes for girls, not boys; the link to connectedness was only marginal. No causal claim.
- Sporefall: weak, sex-specific evidence for family benefit. Do not cite it as showing co-play improves family bonds in general. The abstract reports the associations "for girls only", so it gives no support for boys (the nephews).

- Sporefall: supports framing the game's value as shared family time, with the caveat that it is cross-sectional parent self-report. The "game features that facilitate family relationships" from their open-ended questions were not read (full text not opened).

#### Voida, A., & Greenberg, S. (2009). Wii All Play: The Console Game as a Computational Meeting Place. In Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '09), Boston, pp. 1559-1568. ACM. doi:10.1145/1518701.1518940

## 4. Game feel and juice

### Juul, J., & Begy, J. S. (2016). Good Feedback for bad Players? A preliminary Study of 'juicy' Interface feedback. Poster, Proceedings of the First Joint FDG/DiGRA Conference, Dundee (Abertay University), 1-5 Aug 2016.
- Opened: https://adk.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/good-feedback-for-bad-players-a-preliminary-study-of-juicy-interf/ (Royal Danish Academy research portal: title, authors, Aug 2016, First joint FDG/DiGRA Conference, Dundee) and the poster PDF http://www.jesperjuul.net/text/juiciness.pdf (1 page; header "Proceedings of first joint FDG/DiGRA Conference, Dundee 2016"). Note: the portal lists the second author as "Jason, B."; the PDF gives "Jason Scott Begy". Preliminary poster, not a full paper.
- Method/sample: two mechanically identical versions of a matching-tile game (minimal feedback vs. redundant "juicy" particles and sounds); "We recruited 46 players among the student population at a university"; 6 minutes of play, 50/50 split; ratings on 1-5 scales.
- Numbers (verbatim from poster): ease of use basic 4.57 vs juicy 4.43; mean score "49,682 on the basic version, but a lower 40,340 on the juicy version (correlation -0.24), suggesting that juiciness decreases performance, though not statistically significant in this study (p=.12 ...)"; quality rating basic 3.26 vs juicy 3.74 (p=.20); feeling "clever" 2.83 vs 3.05 (p=.44). "No hypotheses were confirmed".
- Quote: "the excessive feedback of juiciness could be hypothesized to split the attention of a user, increasing cognitive load (Kalyuga, Chandler, and Sweller 1999) and making players perform worse."
- Quote: "Juiciness increased the subjective rating of the game ... Yet juiciness did not make players experience the game as easier to use, barely made them feel more competent, and made them reach lower scores."
- Paraphrase: juice made the game look better to players but tended (non-significantly) to lower performance; the authors link this to cognitive load.
- Sporefall: juice is not free. Decorative effects compete for attention with the information kids need (boss tells, what their build did). Spend the effect budget on meaningful events (reaction triggered, weak spot hit), not constant noise.

### Kao, D. (2020). The effects of juiciness in an action RPG. Entertainment Computing, 34, 100359. doi:10.1016/j.entcom.2020.100359
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.entcom.2020.100359 (title, Dominic Kao, Entertainment Computing vol. 34, article 100359, May 2020). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1016/j.entcom.2020.100359. ScienceDirect full text (listed as open access) was behind a CAPTCHA and was not opened.
- Method/sample (abstract): "a study (N = 3018) in which we compare four identical versions of an action role-playing game with varying amounts of juiciness: (1) None; (2) Medium; (3) High; and (4) Extreme."
- Quote (abstract): "We find that both None and Extreme amounts of juiciness lead to significantly decreased play time, significantly decreased player experience, significantly decreased intrinsic motivation, and significantly decreased performance relative to both Medium and High."
- Paraphrase: an inverted U. Some juice beats none, but extreme juice is as bad as none on play time, experience, motivation and performance. This is the clearest "too much juice hurts" finding in the lane. Participant ages, recruitment and effect sizes were not seen (full text not opened), so do not assume it covers children.
- Sporefall: tune effects to the "Medium/High" band and treat stacked effects (frost plus fire plus lightning plus pierce all firing at once, on a small phone screen) as a risk of drifting into "Extreme". Budget screen shake and particles per event, and cap them when many trigger together.

### Hicks, K., Gerling, K., Dickinson, P., & Vanden Abeele, V. (2019). Juicy Game Design: Understanding the Impact of Visual Embellishments on Player Experience. In Proceedings of the Annual Symposium on Computer-Human Interaction in Play (CHI PLAY '19), Barcelona, pp. 185-197. ACM. doi:10.1145/3311350.3347171
- Note the author order: Hicks, Gerling, Dickinson, Vanden Abeele (not "Hicks, Dickinson, Gerling").
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3311350.3347171 (opened in a browser: title, four authors, CHI PLAY '19, pp. 185-197, published 17 Oct 2019). Cross-check: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3311350.3347171. Full text is PDF-only on ACM and was not read.
- Method/sample (abstract): study 1, 40 participants, two research games (Frogger-clone "Cuber" and FPS "Dungeon Descent"); study 2, 32 participants, Quake 3 Arena.
- Quote (abstract): juiciness "refers to the provision of redundant feedback in situations where a single player action triggers multiple non-functional reactions."
- Quote (abstract): "Results show that visual embellishments contribute to the visual appeal of all games, but only affects aspects such as competence under specific circumstances."
- Paraphrase: juice reliably improves how a game looks, not reliably how competent players feel. Consistent with Juul & Begy.
- Sporefall: visual flair will not by itself fix "kids could not read what their build did". Legibility needs functional feedback (what hit, what status applied, why damage spiked), with juice layered on top.

### Hicks, K., Dickinson, P., Holopainen, J., & Gerling, K. (2018). Good Game Feel: An Empirically Grounded Framework for Juicy Design. In Proceedings of DiGRA 2018: The Game is the Message. Digital Games Research Association. doi:10.26503/dl.v2018i1.936
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.26503/dl.v2018i1.936 (title, four authors, DiGRA 2018). Full text opened: https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/936/936 (17 pp.; header "Proceedings of DiGRA 2018"; the PDF misprints the third author's first name as "Juicy").
- Method/sample: online survey of 17 game developers; affinity diagram; framework refined by applying it to two commercial games. Developer opinion, not player outcomes.
- Framework questions (verbatim): "B6. Overwhelming: Does the game overwhelm or distract by offering too much game information?" (initial version); final version includes "C3. Unambiguous: Can information be connected to actions and only interpreted in one way?" and "A4. Feedback Coherence: Does feedback reflect the importance of the event?"
- Quote (developers on redundancy): "This redundant or perhaps superfluous characteristic of juicy design can be challenging when it overwhelms the player, with one respondent commenting that \"The pleasure aspects should not detract from the others like too much screenshake.\""
- Quote: "Juice alone isn't enough" (developer response).
- Sporefall: use the framework as a checklist for mod feedback. "Unambiguous" (each effect maps to one cause) and "Feedback Coherence" (bigger events get bigger effects) directly target the build-legibility problem; a wet-plus-lightning reaction should be the loudest thing on screen because it matters most.

### Swink, S. (2009; released late 2008). Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation. Burlington, MA: Morgan Kaufmann (Elsevier); now distributed by CRC Press. 376 pp. ISBN 978-0-12-374328-2.
- Year note: the brief says 2008. The publisher page shows "Copyright 2009", Open Library lists publish date 2009, and Juul & Begy (2016) cite it as Swink 2009. Retailer listings show an Oct/Nov 2008 release. Cite as 2009 (or "2008 [©2009]").
- Metadata confirmed at: https://www.routledge.com/Game-Feel-A-Game-Designers-Guide-to-Virtual-Sensation/Swink/p/book/9780123743282 (title, Steve Swink, "Copyright 2009", 376 pages, CRC Press) and https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780123743282.json (publishers Morgan Kaufmann Publishers / CRC Press, 2009, 376 pp.).
- The book text was not opened. The quotes below are from Swink's earlier article on the same framework: Swink, S. (2007, November 23). Game Feel: The Secret Ingredient. Game Developer (Gamasutra). https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-feel-the-secret-ingredient (datePublished 2007-11-23 in page metadata).
- Quote (2007 article, his six components): "Input -- How the player can express their intent to the system. Response -- How the system processes, modifies, and responds to player input in real time. Context -- How constraints give spatial meaning to motion. Polish -- The interactive impression of physicality created by the harmony of animation, sounds, and effects with input-driven motion. Metaphor -- ... Rules -- ..."
- Quote (2007 article): "Polish can include sprays or dustings of particles where things hit or interact, screen shake, view angle shifts, or the squash and stretch of objects colliding. The point is to convey the physical properties of objects through their motion".
- Quote (2007 article): game feel is "mostly subconscious, a combination of sights, sounds, and instant response to action."
- The commonly quoted book definition ("real-time control of virtual objects in a simulated space, with interactions emphasized by polish") was not found on any page I opened; do not quote it as verified.
- Sporefall: Swink frames polish as conveying physical properties, i.e. information. For mods, frost should look and feel heavy and slow, lightning snappy and chaining, so the build reads through motion and sound, not only through numbers.

### Kao, D., Ballou, N., Gerling, K., Breitsohl, H., & Deterding, S. (2024). How does Juicy Game Feedback Motivate? Testing Curiosity, Competence, and Effectance. In Proceedings of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '24), pp. 1-16. ACM. doi:10.1145/3613904.3642656
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3613904.3642656 (title, five authors, CHI '24, 2024-05-11). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642656 (gold open access; full text not read). Preprint: doi:10.31234/osf.io/f83dp.
- Method/sample (abstract): "In a pre-registered (n=1,699) online experiment ... Using a dedicated action RPG and a 2x2+control design, we varied feedback amplification, success-dependence, and variability".
- Quote (abstract): "Structural equation models show curiosity as the strongest enjoyment and only playtime predictor and support theorised competence pathways. Success dependence enhanced all motives, while amplification unexpectedly reduced them, possibly because the tested condition unintentionally impeded players' sense of agency."
- Quote (abstract): the results suggest "that prior juicy game feel guidance ties to legible action-outcome bindings and graded success as preconditions of positive 'low-level' user experience."
- Paraphrase: juice helps when it tracks how well the player did (success-dependent) and keeps action-to-outcome links legible. Amplification alone reduced motivation in this study.
- Sporefall: the strongest single citation for the build-legibility problem. Make feedback scale with outcome (a well-timed wet-plus-lightning combo gets a bigger, distinct payoff than a plain hit), and keep each effect bound to the action that caused it. Bigger for its own sake is not supported.

## 5. Cognitive load and children

### Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257-285. doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4
- Metadata confirmed at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4 (title, John Sweller, Cognitive Science vol. 12, issue 2, pp. 257-285, April 1988, Wiley). Abstract from https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4. Full text not opened (closed access).
- Quote (abstract): "conventional problem solving in the form of means-ends analysis requires a relatively large amount of cognitive processing capacity which is consequently unavailable for schema acquisition. A computational model and experimental evidence provide support for this contention."
- Quote (abstract): "domain specific knowledge in the form of schemas is the primary factor distinguishing experts from novices in problem-solving skill."
- Paraphrase: working out a problem under load can crowd out learning the underlying pattern. Novices lack schemas, so they pay more. Research on adults and students in problem-solving tasks, not games.
- Sporefall: a kid solving "which mods do I combine?" in the middle of a fast fight is doing means-ends search under time pressure, which leaves little capacity to learn what the build does. Move the learning to calm moments (draft screen previews, a pre-boss "weak to lightning" card) and keep in-fight decisions small.

### Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(1), 87-114; discussion 114-185. doi:10.1017/S0140525X01003922. PMID 11515286.
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=11515286&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PubMed). Cross-check: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/s0140525x01003922.
- Quote (abstract): "A single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated along with other, noncapacity-limited sources."
- Quote (abstract): Miller's seven "was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit. Others have since suggested that there is a more precise capacity limit, but that it is only three to five chunks."
- Paraphrase: adults hold about 4 chunks (range 3-5) when they cannot rehearse or group items. Adult data; see Cowan (2016) for children.
- Sporefall: even an adult can track only about four independent things at once. Four mods plus boss state plus a teammate plus position already exceeds that unless mods merge into a single readable "build identity" chunk (e.g., "storm gun" rather than four separate effects).

### Gathercole, S. E., Pickering, S. J., Ambridge, B., & Wearing, H. (2004). The Structure of Working Memory From 4 to 15 Years of Age. Developmental Psychology, 40(2), 177-190. doi:10.1037/0012-1649.40.2.177. PMID 14979759.
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=14979759&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PubMed). Full text opened: https://personalpages.manchester.ac.uk/staff/ben.ambridge/papers/Gathercole,%20Pickering,%20Ambridge%20&%20Waring%20(2004).pdf (co-author's page; 14 pp.).
- Method/sample: "Over 700 children were assessed" with the Working Memory Test Battery for Children; five schools in southwest England; after exclusions, group sizes 4-5 yrs n=144, 6-7 yrs n=184, 8-9 yrs n=154, 10-11 yrs n=132, 13-15 yrs n=105 (18 excluded).
- Quote (abstract): "The results indicate that the basic modular structure of working memory is present from 6 years of age and possibly earlier, with each component undergoing sizable expansion in functional capacity throughout the early and middle school years to adolescence."
- Quote (results): "All nine tests yielded broadly similar developmental functions, with performance increasing linearly from 4 to 14 years in general and leveling off between 14 and 15 years. The only marked departure from this profile was observed for the Visual Patterns Test, on which scores reached an asymptotic level at 11 years."
- Paraphrase: children have the adult structure of working memory by about 6, but capacity keeps growing roughly linearly until about 14. An 8-year-old and a 12-year-old differ substantially. The paper reports standardised (z) scores, not item counts.
- Sporefall: the 8-12 band spans a large capacity gap. Design for the 8-year-old: fewer simultaneous things to track, and cues that do not need to be held in memory (persistent on-screen status on the boss, not a one-time popup).

### Cowan, N. (2016). Working Memory Maturation: Can We Get at the Essence of Cognitive Growth? Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(2), 239-264. doi:10.1177/1745691615621279. PMID 26993277; PMCID PMC4800832.
- Metadata and abstract confirmed at: https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=26993277&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PubMed). Full text (author manuscript) opened: https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=PMC4800832&retmode=xml (PMC).
- Quote (full text): "Estimates of working memory capacity from such procedures are typically in the range of 3 or 4 objects in adults (Cowan, 2001; Luck & Vogel, 1998), with smaller estimates in preschoolers and children in the early elementary school years, about 2 to 2.5 items".
- Quote (abstract): "Many investigators have come to suspect that working memory capacity may be constant after infancy because of various factors such as developmental increases in knowledge, filtering out of irrelevant distractions, encoding and rehearsal strategies, and pattern formation. With each of these factors controlled, though, working memory still improves during the school years."
- Finding (full text, summarising Cowan et al., 2010): with children aged 7-8, 12-13 and college students, "With only 4 items in an array (2 circles, 2 triangles), participants of all age groups filtered out less-relevant shapes equally. Yet, the younger children remembered far fewer items from the arrays." Cowan reads this as capacity itself growing, not just better filtering.
- Paraphrase: young school-age children hold about 2 to 2.5 items versus 3 to 4 in adults, and the gap is real capacity, not just knowledge or strategy.
- Sporefall: for a 7-8 year old, "what does my build do" plus "what is the boss about to do" may already fill working memory. Put the boss telegraph in the world where the eye already is (on the boss, big and colour-coded), and show mod combos as one fused effect, so neither has to be held in memory.

## Cross-cutting takeaways for Sporefall (from the verified sources above)

- Telegraph every boss attack and make weakness visible in the fight (Stout 2015; Keren 2018). Failure then reads as fair and fixable, which is what makes it motivating (Juul 2013; Petralito et al. 2017; Frommel et al. 2021).
- Legibility beats volume. Juice improves looks but not reliably competence (Hicks et al. 2019; Juul & Begy 2016). Extreme juice is as bad as none (Kao 2020), and plain amplification reduced motivation while success-dependent feedback raised it (Kao et al. 2024).
- Children aged 7-8 hold about 2 to 2.5 items versus 3 to 4 for adults, and capacity grows until about 14 (Cowan 2016; Gathercole et al. 2004; Cowan 2001). Put information on the boss, fuse mod combos into one readable effect, and move decisions out of the fight (Sweller 1988).
- Co-located co-play raises fun and competence (Gajadhar et al. 2008). Cross-player ability synergies, complementary roles and shared targets drive cooperation (Rocha et al. 2008; Aghabeigi 2011; Beznosyk et al. 2012). Sequential combos (wet, then zap) are the easy form of interdependence for kids (Harris et al. 2016).
- Give each family member a role matched to maturity, with a shared goal that needs talk (Takeuchi & Stevens 2011). Mixed-skill groups mentor naturally but prefer "both chopping stuff" to one player being "the helper" (Voida & Greenberg 2009).
